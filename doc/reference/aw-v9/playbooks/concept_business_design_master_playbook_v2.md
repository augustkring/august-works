# Concept & Business Design Master Playbook - V2.0 Validated Golden Standard
## Evidence-informed standards for turning an opportunity into a clear, coherent, differentiated and testable business concept

**Version:** 2.0 - Validated Golden Standard  
**Research cutoff:** 22 September 2026  
**Scope:** Concept and business design. From a raw opportunity, idea, technology, customer problem, existing product or strategic challenge to a clearly defined value proposition and business model that is ready for market validation.  
**Backbone:** Strategyzer Business Model Canvas + Value Proposition Canvas.  
**Document type:** Evergreen business-design playbook. Not a full market-validation playbook, product-development playbook, go-to-market plan, corporate strategy process or five-year business plan.  
**Operator-neutral:** The standards apply whether work is performed by founders, innovation teams, consultants, product teams, executives, software or AI-assisted workflows.

---

# Executive doctrine

Business design is not the act of filling in a canvas. It is the disciplined design of a coherent system for **creating meaningful value for specific customers and stakeholders, delivering that value, and capturing enough value to sustain the organization**. The Business Model Canvas is used here as the common visual language for that system; the Value Proposition Canvas is the deeper design tool for the customer-value interface. This interpretation is consistent with Strategyzer and with academic business-model research that describes business models as architectures or activity systems for value creation, delivery and capture. [S01][S02][S15][S16]

There is no scientifically established single best innovation process. That is not a weakness in the discipline; it is a property of front-end innovation under uncertainty. ISO 56007:2023 explicitly covers the front-end activities of identifying opportunities, creating concepts and validating them, while stating that **no one method or set of tools is appropriate in all situations**. Stanford d.school likewise cautions against reducing design to one fixed recipe, and systematic business-model-innovation reviews find interconnected, heterogeneous processes rather than one universal sequence. [S12][S23][S33]

The gold standard for this playbook is therefore **not one rigid funnel**. It is a robust operating system: a shared backbone, evidence hygiene, customer and stakeholder specificity, deliberate exploration of alternatives, system-level business-model design, explicit economic and environmental logic, and a clean handoff to market validation.

The core operating loop is:

```text
FRAME THE DESIGN CHALLENGE
-> BUILD AN EVIDENCE + CONTEXT PACK
-> MAP THE BUSINESS-MODEL ENVIRONMENT
-> MAP CUSTOMER / STAKEHOLDER ECOSYSTEM
-> BUILD PRIORITY CUSTOMER PROFILES
-> MAKE VALUE CONCRETE WITH VALUE SCENES
-> DESIGN MULTIPLE VALUE PROPOSITIONS
-> DESIGN MULTIPLE BUSINESS-MODEL ARCHITECTURES
-> STRESS-TEST ACTIVITY SYSTEM, INCENTIVES, COMPETITION AND ECONOMICS
-> ASSESS DESIGN QUALITY WITHOUT CONFUSING IT WITH MARKET EVIDENCE
-> EXTRACT AND PRIORITIZE ASSUMPTIONS
-> SELECT / RECOMBINE A TESTABLE CONCEPT ARCHITECTURE
-> WRITE THE CONCEPT DEFINITION
-> HAND OFF TO MARKET VALIDATION
```

The loop is intentionally iterative. It is normal to move backward when a downstream design choice exposes a weak upstream assumption.

## The one-sentence rule

> **Concept & Business Design decides what, for whom, how and why the business is supposed to work. Market Validation decides whether reality agrees.**

A business concept is therefore **ready** when it is coherent, explicit, ethically and legally admissible enough to test, and falsifiable - not when the team feels confident that it will succeed.

# Scope boundaries

## This playbook DOES

- Turn an opportunity, idea, technology, customer problem or existing product into a concrete business concept.
- Define customer segments and relevant stakeholder roles.
- Design and prioritize customer jobs, pains and gains.
- Design one or more value propositions.
- Design one or more Business Model Canvases.
- Explore business-model patterns and alternative architectures.
- Examine competitive alternatives and customer-perceived differentiation.
- Map market, industry, trend, regulatory and macro context.
- Perform first-principles plausibility checks on revenue, cost, capability and delivery logic.
- Surface contradictions, dependencies, critical assumptions and unknowns.
- Decide whether a concept should be redesigned, parked, dropped for a hard reason, or moved into market validation.
- Produce the exact inputs the Market Validation & Pretotyping Playbook needs.

## This playbook DOES NOT

- Prove product-market fit.
- Treat customer interviews as proof that customers will buy.
- Run fake doors, pretotypes, MVPs or demand experiments.
- Design the detailed product backlog or technical architecture.
- Build launch plans, campaign plans or sales playbooks.
- Produce detailed pricing optimization.
- Produce a full financial model, funding model or business plan.
- Decide a complete corporate strategy.
- Declare an idea a market winner based on internal design quality.

Those jobs belong to downstream or adjacent playbooks.

## Evergreen core vs current implementation layer

**Evergreen core:** customer/stakeholder value, BMC/VPC logic, activity-system coherence, alternative generation, value creation/delivery/capture, rough economics, environment, evidence hygiene, assumptions and readiness for validation.

**Current implementation layer:** exact Strategyzer playbook names, current risk terminology, AI tooling, regulatory details, live market data and institution-specific workshop formats. These should be refreshed when the playbook is executed if they materially affect the concept.

A changing tool name must never become a permanent innovation law.

# Evidence standard and source hierarchy

Business design mixes **standards**, **normative tools** (how practitioners recommend designing), **descriptive/empirical research** (how business models operate and which design properties have been associated with outcomes), and **context-specific evidence**. Those are different kinds of knowledge and must not be conflated.

## Source hierarchy

| Tier | Source type | Use in this playbook |
|---|---|---|
| **A0** | International standards / authoritative definitions | Defines general front-end innovation principles and boundaries without prescribing one universal method; especially ISO 56000/56007. |
| **A1** | Primary framework creators and official methods | Defines canonical use of BMC, VPC, Business Model Environment, Strategyzer risk categories and established design-thinking methods. |
| **A2** | Peer-reviewed research and systematic reviews | Supports claims about business-model architecture, fit, activity systems, exploration, external search, process diversity and limits of the field. |
| **B** | Major research-led institutions and established practitioner frameworks | Adds complementary lenses: IDEO, Stanford d.school/Lean LaunchPad, MIT, HBS/Wharton/INSEAD, Blue Ocean Strategy. |
| **C** | Practitioner cases and field heuristics | Inspiration and trigger questions. Never universal proof. |
| **D** | Internal/company/customer evidence | Most relevant to the specific concept, but must be tagged by evidence type, scope and strength. |

## Evidence labels inside the work

Every important sticky note, statement or design claim should be classifiable as one of these:

- `OBSERVED` - directly observed in customer, user, operational or market reality.
- `PRIMARY_DATA` - company data, behavioral data, transaction data, usage data, interview/observation material.
- `SECONDARY_EVIDENCE` - credible external market, regulatory, technical or industry evidence.
- `INFERENCE` - a conclusion derived from evidence, but not directly observed.
- `ASSUMPTION` - something that must be true for the concept to work but is not sufficiently evidenced.
- `DESIGN_CHOICE` - a deliberate choice made by the team, not a fact about the world.
- `CONSTRAINT` - a non-negotiable technical, regulatory, strategic, contractual, ethical or resource boundary.
- `UNKNOWN` - an unresolved issue that has not yet been converted into a precise assumption.

### Golden rule

**Never allow a design choice, framework suggestion, benchmark or AI-generated output to silently become a customer fact.**

# The 30 Golden Standards

1. **Use the Business Model Canvas as the backbone, not as a checklist.** The blocks must reinforce one another as a system. [S01][S16]
2. **Use the Value Proposition Canvas to zoom into each materially different customer segment.** One generic profile across different jobs, buying roles or economics destroys precision. [S02]
3. **Map the customer/stakeholder ecosystem before defaulting to the end user.** User, beneficiary, buyer, economic buyer, decision maker, channel partner and blocker may be different actors. [S35]
4. **Separate the customer profile from the value map.** Jobs, pains and gains describe the customer; products, pain relievers and gain creators are design choices. [S02]
5. **Start with customer/stakeholder value, not the novelty of the technology.** A technically impressive product is not automatically a valuable proposition. [S07][S30]
6. **Treat functional, social and emotional jobs - and the circumstance in which they arise - as potentially relevant.** Do not reduce value to functional features. [S02][S14]
7. **Prioritize and evidence-tag the customer profile.** Great concepts focus on a small number of important jobs, pains and gains rather than claiming to solve everything. [S07][S37]
8. **Make value concrete before polishing language.** Use value scenes, workflows or before/after moments to show exactly when and how value is created. [S36]
9. **Design differentiation through the customer’s eyes and against real alternatives.** Feature difference without meaningful customer-perceived value is weak differentiation. [S07][S25]
10. **Design at the business-model level, not only at the product level.** Value can be created through activities, channels, relationships, partners, revenue logic and cost structure. [S16]
11. **Treat the business model as an activity and incentive system.** Make activity content, structure, governance, dependencies and stakeholder incentives explicit when they matter. [S16]
12. **Create multiple materially different concept architectures before converging.** Prematurely polishing one canvas creates fixation and false certainty. Parallel exploration has supportive empirical evidence in design contexts. [S13]
13. **Search outside the team and outside the category.** External knowledge search can expand the business-model design space and reduce local fixation. [S32]
14. **Use business-model and value-proposition patterns as structured prompts, not recipes.** Patterns expand the search space; context determines fit. [S08][S39]
15. **Check the environment and strategic interaction outside the canvas.** Market forces, industry forces, trends, macro conditions and competitor/partner responses can invalidate an otherwise coherent model. [S05][S19]
16. **Include regulation, trust, safety and foreseeable negative externalities early.** They are design inputs, not a compliance or ethics afterthought. [S11][S41]
17. **Distinguish business model from strategy and tactics.** They interact, but they are not interchangeable. [S18][S19]
18. **Make value creation, value delivery and value capture explicit.** A concept that explains only the offer is not a complete business model. [S15]
19. **Do first-principles economic plausibility before detailed forecasting.** A five-year spreadsheet cannot rescue structurally impossible unit economics.
20. **Treat the revenue model as a design variable.** How customers pay changes adoption, incentives, cash flow, risk and sometimes the value proposition itself.
21. **Do not assume recurring revenue, platform models, self-service or automation are inherently superior.** They are patterns whose value depends on customer behavior and system economics.
22. **For multi-sided businesses, design every critical side and the participation loop.** A marketplace is not one customer segment with a second segment added later.
23. **For B2B, separate stakeholder roles when their jobs, objections or value cases differ.** One person may hold several roles, but the model must not assume they are identical. [S35]
24. **Mark every important claim by evidence status and provenance.** A beautiful canvas can still be 90% assumptions.
25. **Treat interviews, observations and desk research as discovery evidence for the claims they actually measure - not as automatic proof of demand.** Downstream validation tests behavior and commitment. [S29][S37]
26. **Use the four risk lenses before validation: desirability, feasibility, viability and survivability.** [S04]
27. **Use design assessments and scores diagnostically, never as market evidence or a predictive success score.** A score can reveal weak design blocks; it cannot prove the market wants the concept. [S38]
28. **A concept is ready for validation when it is specific, internally coherent and falsifiable.** Vagueness is not optionality.
29. **Do not kill a concept merely because evidence is missing.** Missing evidence normally means “test”; kill or park only for a hard design, constraint, strategic, responsibility or portfolio reason.
30. **The final deliverable is a coherent concept plus a prioritized uncertainty map.** The validation team should never have to guess what the concept is or what must be true for it to work.

# Part I - What business design actually is

## 1.1 Business models are representations of a system

Academic research does not contain one universally accepted definition of a business model. A major Academy of Management review identified at least three broad interpretations: the business model as an attribute of a real organization, as a managerial/cognitive schema, and as a formal conceptual representation. Systematic reviews also note construct ambiguity and fragmented evidence in the business-model-innovation field. [S21][S22]

This playbook therefore makes a practical choice:

> **Use the Business Model Canvas as the shared representation of the business, while remembering that the real business model is the real system of activities, relationships, transactions and economics behind the drawing.**

Teece frames the model around value creation, delivery and capture. Zott and Amit deepen this with an activity-system view: the model includes the content of activities, their structure, their governance and their interdependence across firm boundaries. [S15][S16]

## 1.2 The Business Model Canvas is the backbone

The Strategyzer Business Model Canvas describes the logic of the business with nine connected blocks: Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships and Cost Structure. Strategyzer explicitly warns that a filled-in canvas is not itself a decision or strategy; the value lies in using the canvas to challenge, redesign and compare alternatives. Its current toolkit also groups model risk around desirability, feasibility, viability and the environment-facing dimension. [S01][S03]

We use it because it is:

- simple enough for cross-functional teams,
- broad enough to capture the whole business logic,
- compatible with Value Proposition Design,
- compatible with assumption mapping and downstream testing,
- visual enough to compare alternative models quickly.

We do **not** claim that the Business Model Canvas is empirically proven to be superior to every other business-model representation. The academic literature does not establish such a universal ranking. Strategyzer is the operating backbone because it is coherent, widely used and integrates well with the rest of the innovation system. [S21][S22]

## 1.3 The Value Proposition Canvas is the customer-value backbone

The Value Proposition Canvas zooms into the connection between one Customer Segment and one Value Proposition. The customer side describes jobs, pains and gains. The value side describes products/services, pain relievers and gain creators. Strategyzer’s current guidance emphasizes that fit is a claim until customers confirm it. [S02]

The operational implication is important:

```text
CUSTOMER PROFILE = what we believe/observe about the customer's reality
VALUE MAP        = what we choose to design
FIT              = the hypothesized match between the two
VALIDATED FIT    = a downstream evidence question
```

## 1.4 Business design is an exploration problem

IDEO describes design thinking as the integration of desirability, feasibility and viability. Stanford d.school emphasizes abilities such as navigating ambiguity, learning from others, synthesizing information, rapidly experimenting and moving between concrete and abstract levels rather than prescribing one universal step sequence. [S11][S12]

For business design, that means the team should be comfortable holding multiple plausible configurations at once. The goal is not to guess the final answer early; the goal is to create a small set of increasingly coherent hypotheses that can later face reality.

## 1.5 Front-end innovation is a discipline, not one prescribed method

ISO 56007:2023 is particularly useful as an external sanity check on the scope of this playbook. It places **identifying opportunities, creating concepts and validating them** inside front-end innovation and explicitly notes that there is no single method or toolset for all situations. ISO 56000:2025 likewise applies across product, service, process and business-model innovation and across market-, user-, technology- and design-driven approaches. [S33][S34]

The practical implication is:

```text
STANDARDIZE THE QUESTIONS AND EVIDENCE DISCIPLINE
NOT THE ANSWER OR A SINGLE CREATIVE ROUTE
```

Use Strategyzer as the shared operating language, then adapt the sequence and supporting lenses to the uncertainty actually present.

# Part II - The Concept & Business Design Operating Loop

# 2. Frame the design challenge

Every business-design project begins with a **design frame**, not a canvas.

A design frame states:

1. **Why are we doing this?**
2. **What kind of opportunity are we exploring?**
3. **What is in scope?**
4. **What is explicitly out of scope?**
5. **Which constraints are real?**
6. **Which outcomes would make the effort strategically worthwhile?**
7. **Which decisions should this phase enable?**

## 2.1 Typical starting points

The process can legitimately start from many places:

- an observed customer problem,
- a new technology,
- a regulatory change,
- an underused internal asset,
- a market or demographic shift,
- a strategic capability,
- a new distribution channel,
- an existing product entering a new segment or country,
- a cost or operational breakthrough,
- a partner opportunity,
- a founder insight or mission.

**Do not force every innovation story to pretend it started with a customer interview.** Technology-push and capability-led ideas are legitimate. The discipline is that they must eventually translate into customer/stakeholder value and a coherent capture model. IDEO’s desirability-feasibility-viability model and the broader business-model literature both support looking across multiple dimensions rather than treating one origin as universally superior. [S11][S15]

## 2.2 Design challenge format

```yaml
design_challenge:
  trigger: "What changed / what opportunity are we seeing?"
  strategic_intent: "What are we trying to create or improve?"
  unit_of_analysis: "venture | product-service | business line | market entry | model redesign"
  initial_customer_or_context: "..."
  non_negotiable_constraints:
    - "..."
  explicit_out_of_scope:
    - "..."
  decision_needed: "What decision should business design enable?"
  time_horizon: "..."
```

### Quality gate

A good frame is broad enough to permit alternatives but narrow enough to guide work. "How might we use AI?" is usually too solution-led and broad. "How might we reduce the cost and delay of monthly management reporting for 20-200 employee firms without requiring an ERP replacement?" creates a more useful design space.

# 3. Build the evidence and context pack

Business design should not begin from a blank whiteboard if relevant evidence already exists.

## 3.1 The evidence pack

Collect only enough material to improve the design. Do not let research become a substitute for making choices.

### Customer and user evidence

- Existing customer interviews and discovery notes
- Contextual observation or workflow shadowing
- Sales-call transcripts
- Support tickets and complaints
- Win/loss notes
- Customer success notes
- Product usage data
- Search behavior and internal site search
- Reviews and public customer commentary
- Churn/cancellation reasons
- Existing purchase and usage patterns

### Commercial evidence

- Actual prices paid
- Discounting behavior
- Deal cycle length
- Sales objections
- Gross margin and service cost
- Retention/repeat purchase
- Channel economics
- Contract structures

### Market and environment evidence

- Competitor and alternative offerings
- Business models used by adjacent and analogous industries
- Regulatory requirements
- Technology capability and cost trends
- Supply constraints
- Market structure
- Distribution power
- Category conventions
- Macro conditions relevant to the model
- Counterevidence and failure cases that challenge the current thesis

### Internal evidence

- Existing capabilities
- Proprietary assets/data
- Brand access
- Partnerships
- Installed base
- Distribution relationships
- Operational strengths/constraints

## 3.2 Search wide enough to escape the current category

Do not restrict discovery to direct competitors. Recent business-model research finds that external knowledge search is relevant to business-model innovation and that different search breadth/depth patterns are associated with different forms of business-model change. The finding is not a formula for success, but it supports a strong design default: deliberately inspect analogous models, adjacent industries, substitutes and external capabilities before converging. [S32]

Minimum external-search set for meaningful innovation work:

- direct competitors,
- substitutes and “do nothing”,
- one structurally similar business in another category,
- one radically different model solving an analogous coordination/economic problem,
- one credible failure or abandoned model.

The goal is not copying. It is enlarging the design space and discovering assumptions the current category takes for granted.

## 3.3 Evidence quality is contextual

A customer interview can provide strong evidence that a workflow is painful or that a procurement rule exists. It is weak evidence that the customer will pay EUR 200 per month for a proposed solution. A CRM can provide strong evidence about sales cycle and win/loss patterns, but weak evidence about a future category that has never been sold.

The question is not simply "Is this source strong?" but:

> **Strong evidence for which claim?**

## 3.4 Discovery is not validation

This playbook uses qualitative and secondary evidence to **shape** the concept. It does not convert stated interest into validated demand. That distinction is intentionally strict.

Strategyzer separates discovery from stronger validation evidence, and its VPC guidance states that designed fit remains a claim until customers confirm it. Stanford Lean LaunchPad similarly treats customer, partner and competitor conversations as part of evidence-based entrepreneurship and iterative assumption testing rather than a substitute for the market itself. [S02][S29][S40]

## 3.5 Evidence register

```yaml
claim_id: C-017
claim: "Finance managers spend several hours every month reconciling management reporting manually"
status: OBSERVED
source_type: "customer interview + workflow observation"
source_count: 7
segment: "Danish SMEs, 20-200 employees"
confidence_for_this_claim: medium
limitations:
  - "participants recruited from existing network"
  - "does not prove willingness to pay"
implication: "time-saving may be a high-value job/pain"
```

# 4. Map the business-model environment

Strategyzer’s Business Model Environment explicitly places the canvas inside four contextual areas: Market Forces, Industry Forces, Key Trends and Macroeconomic Forces. [S05]

Use the environment map before converging on a model, not as a decorative appendix.

## 4.1 Market Forces

Ask:

- Which segments are growing, shrinking or changing?
- Which customer jobs are becoming more important or less important?
- What creates switching behavior?
- Where is willingness or ability to pay changing?
- Which alternatives are gaining adoption?
- What market frictions are structural vs temporary?

## 4.2 Industry Forces

Ask:

- Who controls distribution?
- Which suppliers or complements are powerful?
- Which platforms or standards constrain the model?
- Where are substitutes emerging?
- Which partners could become competitors?
- Which competitors have structural cost, data or network advantages?

## 4.3 Key Trends

Ask:

- Technology changes
- Regulatory changes
- Social and behavioral changes
- Demographic changes
- Labor/capability changes
- Climate/resource changes if relevant
- AI and automation effects

## 4.4 Macroeconomic Forces

Ask:

- Capital availability and cost
- Inflation/interest rates
- Currency exposure
- Commodity/energy cost
- Labor cost
- Supply availability
- Purchasing-power shifts

## 4.5 Why-now statement

Every concept should end this step with a compact "why now":

```text
This concept is becoming possible/necessary now because [change],
which creates [new customer pain/opportunity], while [capability/trend]
makes a different business model plausible.
```

A why-now statement is a design thesis, not proof.

## 4.6 Translate context into design implications

An environment scan that ends as a list of trends is incomplete. For each material force, record the design consequence:

| External force | Evidence | Model blocks affected | Opportunity / threat | Assumption created |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New regulation | regulator text | VP, Activities, Costs | both | compliance path is commercially viable |
| Falling AI inference cost | vendor/benchmark data | VP, Activities, Costs | opportunity | saving reaches our workload at target quality |
| Powerful distributor | industry evidence | Channels, Revenue | threat | acceptable access terms are possible |

## 4.7 Strategic interaction check

Business models do not perform in isolation. Research on business-model strategy emphasizes that the value of a model can depend on how it interacts with competitors, partners and other industry actors. [S19]

Before convergence ask:

- If this works, who has an incentive and ability to copy, bundle, block or disintermediate it?
- Which partner becomes more powerful if the model scales?
- Does our model improve or worsen the economics of critical complementors?
- Does a competitor have a structurally cheaper response?
- Which advantage disappears if a platform, supplier or model provider changes terms?

# 5. Define customer and stakeholder architecture

The Business Model Canvas asks "for whom are we creating value?" The answer must be specific enough that the value proposition can actually differ by segment. Strategyzer's current customer-ecosystem approach makes the same point operationally: first map the actors who use, benefit, decide, buy, pay, influence or block, then decide which customer deserves primary design focus. [S01][S35]

**Primary focus does not mean only stakeholder.** A concept can optimize around one priority customer while still requiring viable value propositions and incentives for other critical actors.

## 5.1 What counts as a useful customer segment?

A segment is useful when its members are sufficiently similar on the variables that affect the business model, for example:

- job or outcome sought,
- context or trigger,
- pains/gains,
- willingness/ability to pay,
- buying process,
- adoption friction,
- channel access,
- cost to serve,
- regulatory constraints,
- value created.

Demographics or firmographics may matter, but they are usually insufficient on their own. MIT's entrepreneurship curriculum similarly treats segmentation, beachhead selection and end-user profiling as explicit design work rather than assuming a broad market label is enough. [S42]

## 5.2 Jobs-to-be-done as a lens, not a religion

Strategyzer incorporates jobs-to-be-done into the Value Proposition Canvas, and Christensen’s JTBD work emphasizes understanding the progress or outcome customers seek in a particular circumstance rather than relying only on customer attributes. [S02][S14]

Use JTBD to ask:

- What is the customer trying to accomplish?
- In what circumstance?
- What triggers the job?
- What alternatives do they currently "hire"?
- How do they define success?
- What makes the job difficult, risky, expensive or emotionally charged?

Do **not** treat JTBD as proof that conventional segmentation never matters. Geography, regulation, firm size, purchasing power, workflow maturity or role can be genuine constraints.

## 5.3 One customer profile per materially different segment

Do not write:

> "SMEs, enterprises and freelancers want to save time."

Instead create separate profiles if jobs, purchasing processes or value mechanisms differ materially.

## 5.4 B2B stakeholder architecture

In B2B, map roles explicitly:

| Role | Core question |
|---|---|
| User | Who uses the solution? |
| Beneficiary | Who gets the outcome? |
| Champion | Who wants change and pushes internally? |
| Buyer | Who runs the purchase process? |
| Economic buyer | Who owns budget/ROI? |
| Approver | Who can block for legal, security, IT, compliance or procurement reasons? |
| Payer | Which entity actually pays? |

If these actors have materially different jobs, pains or gains, build separate Customer Profiles.

## 5.5 Multi-sided architecture

For marketplaces, platforms and ecosystems, identify each side separately:

```text
Side A value -> participation -> activity -> value for Side B
Side B value -> participation -> activity -> value for Side A
Platform -> rules, trust, matching, payment, governance, data, economics
```

A platform concept is incomplete until the team explains why each side joins before the other side is fully established.

# 6. Build the Customer Profile

For each priority segment, use the Value Proposition Canvas Customer Profile: Jobs, Pains, Gains. [S02]

## 6.1 Jobs

Map what the customer is trying to get done.

### Functional jobs

Tasks, problems, decisions and outcomes.

### Social jobs

How the customer wants to be perceived or positioned by others.

### Emotional/personal jobs

How the customer wants to feel, what they fear, or what confidence/control they seek.

### Supporting jobs

Buying, implementing, learning, maintaining, switching, reporting and disposing can be important jobs even if they are not the core consumption job.

## 6.2 Pains

Pains include:

- cost,
- time,
- effort,
- risk,
- uncertainty,
- complexity,
- failure modes,
- social consequences,
- emotional frustration,
- poor performance of alternatives,
- adoption barriers.

Strategyzer explicitly distinguishes pains from gains; a pain disappearing often just restores the expected baseline rather than creating a gain. [S02]

## 6.3 Gains

Gains include outcomes customers require, expect, desire or would find surprising/delightful:

- financial benefit,
- speed,
- convenience,
- confidence,
- quality,
- status,
- control,
- simplicity,
- reduced risk,
- better outcomes,
- easier adoption.

## 6.4 Rank, do not collect endlessly

For every job, pain and gain, add:

- importance/intensity,
- frequency,
- evidence status,
- segment/context,
- existing alternative,
- consequence if unsolved.

A profile with 40 equally important pains is not a useful design artifact.

## 6.5 Customer Profile quality test

A strong profile should let someone unfamiliar with the solution understand the customer without hearing the product pitch.

Red flags:

- Product features appear on the customer side.
- Pains are just missing product features.
- Jobs are written as "use our product".
- Everything is high priority.
- The profile combines different segments.
- Claims are copied from competitor marketing.
- AI invented customer quotes are treated as evidence.

## 6.6 Refine the Customer Profile with structured discovery

A useful sequence is:

```text
MAP CURRENT HYPOTHESES
-> INTERVIEW / OBSERVE THE RIGHT PEOPLE
-> CONFIRM / REFUTE / EDIT / ADD
-> RE-RANK JOBS, PAINS AND GAINS
-> RECORD SOURCE + SCOPE FOR EACH IMPORTANT CHANGE
```

Strategyzer's 2026 customer-profile interview guidance uses the Customer Profile as a common interview artifact so that findings can be compared across conversations rather than becoming disconnected notes. [S37]

Use this for **customer-understanding evidence**. It does not turn interview agreement into purchase validation. Ask about concrete past/current behavior, context, workarounds and consequences before asking hypothetical future questions.

## 6.7 Create Value Scenes before polishing the proposition

Strategyzer's current Value Scenes method uses simple storyboards to make the customer's moment of need and the before/after experience concrete. [S36]

For each priority value mechanism, create a 3-6 frame scene:

```text
TRIGGER / CONTEXT
-> CURRENT WORKAROUND OR FRICTION
-> PRODUCT / SERVICE INTERACTION
-> MECHANISM OF CHANGE
-> CUSTOMER OUTCOME
-> RESIDUAL FRICTION / TRADE-OFF
```

Value Scenes are especially useful when teams are arguing with adjectives such as "easy", "smart" or "seamless". If the value cannot be shown in a concrete customer situation, the proposition is probably still too vague. The scene is a design artifact, not evidence that the customer values it.

# 7. Design the Value Map

The Value Map contains Products & Services, Pain Relievers and Gain Creators. [S02]

## 7.1 Products and services

Describe the bundle the customer actually receives, not the internal technology stack.

Good:

> "Monthly close assistant that gathers source files, reconciles recurring accounts, flags anomalies and prepares a review package."

Weak:

> "Agentic AI using RAG, vector databases and multi-agent orchestration."

Technology belongs in the explanation only when it creates customer-relevant value, trust, economics or differentiation.

## 7.2 Pain relievers

Specify exactly which pain is reduced and by what mechanism.

```text
Pain: reporting requires manual collection from five systems.
Reliever: automatically retrieves the defined source data and maps it to the reporting structure.
```

## 7.3 Gain creators

Specify which desired outcome is created.

```text
Gain: management report is ready by day 3 with traceable source data.
Creator: standardized close workflow + exception handling + approval trail.
```

## 7.4 Focus is a design choice

Strategyzer’s current guidance on strong value propositions emphasizes focus on a small number of important, underserved jobs/pains/gains and differentiation on dimensions customers care about. [S07]

Do not map every pain to a reliever. Deliberately choose what the offer will **not** solve.

## 7.5 Fit is a hypothesis

Three levels should be kept conceptually separate:

1. **Paper/design fit** - the value map appears to address prioritized jobs/pains/gains.
2. **Problem/solution evidence** - there is evidence that the customer problem and proposed mechanism matter.
3. **Market/business evidence** - actual behavior supports adoption, payment and a viable model.

Strategyzer uses labels such as problem-solution fit, product-market fit and business-model fit, but the exact stage terminology is not universal across the innovation field. Use the terms as useful communication aids, not universal scientific thresholds. [S09][S21][S22]

### Non-standard rule rejected

Do not use a universal rule such as "a value proposition must cover 50-70% of pains/gains." No robust cross-industry evidence supports a fixed percentage as a universal threshold. Focus and importance matter more than coverage count.

## 7.6 Use value-proposition patterns to widen the search space

Strategyzer's current 2026 playbook groups value-creation prompts into six pattern families: **Ease Boosters, Access Boosters, Confidence Builders, Value Catalysts, Identity Builders, and Market Shapers**. [S39]

Use them as structured ideation prompts:

- **Ease:** remove time, effort, friction or complexity.
- **Access:** make something newly reachable, available or usable.
- **Confidence:** reduce uncertainty, risk or lack of trust.
- **Value catalyst:** materially improve economic or functional outcome.
- **Identity:** support belonging, status, self-image or social meaning.
- **Market shaper:** change the basis of choice or how value is created/consumed.

These are **framework patterns, not empirically universal categories or guaranteed winners**. Their job is to prevent teams from defaulting to "faster/cheaper/easier" and to generate meaningfully different value mechanisms.

# 8. Design customer-perceived differentiation

A value proposition must compete against the customer’s available alternatives, not only against a list of direct competitors.

## 8.1 Alternative map

For the priority job, map:

- direct competitors,
- adjacent category solutions,
- internal/manual work,
- outsourcing,
- spreadsheets/tools,
- "do nothing",
- delay,
- self-service,
- incumbent process.

## 8.2 Difference is not differentiation

A feature is strategically relevant only if it changes an outcome or trade-off customers care about.

Strategyzer’s 2026 guidance explicitly emphasizes differentiation on customer-relevant jobs, pains and gains rather than competitor feature comparison. [S07]

## 8.3 Strategy Canvas / Four Actions lens

As an optional complement to the VPC, use Blue Ocean Strategy’s value-innovation logic to ask where the concept should:

- eliminate,
- reduce,
- raise,
- create.

Use customer-perceived factors, not internal feature categories. Strategyzer itself recommends combining the VPC and Strategy Canvas when useful. [S07][S25]

## 8.4 Differentiation statement

```text
For [segment] trying to [job],
we create superior value on [2-3 customer-relevant dimensions]
by [mechanism/business-model choice],
while deliberately doing less on [dimensions that matter less to this segment].
```

A concept that claims to outperform on every dimension is probably not making real trade-offs.

# 9. Design the Business Model Canvas

The canvas should explain the full business logic, not just summarize the product.

# 9.1 Customer Segments

For whom do we create value?

Capture:

- priority segment(s),
- role/stakeholder structure,
- context/trigger,
- relative value potential,
- distinct service/economic requirements.

# 9.2 Value Propositions

What value is created for each segment?

Capture:

- core job/outcome,
- chosen pains/gains,
- product/service bundle,
- mechanism,
- differentiation,
- key proof requirement (as an assumption until validated).

# 9.3 Channels

How does the customer discover, evaluate, buy, receive and use the offer?

Separate:

```text
Awareness -> Evaluation -> Purchase -> Delivery -> Use -> Support/Renewal
```

Channel is not merely marketing. It can materially change economics, trust, adoption and customer experience.

# 9.4 Customer Relationships

What relationship is required?

Examples:

- self-service,
- automated service,
- dedicated account management,
- community,
- co-creation,
- managed service,
- human-in-the-loop support.

Ask whether the relationship logic scales with the economics.

# 9.5 Revenue Streams

How is value captured?

Common structures include:

- one-time transaction,
- recurring subscription,
- usage-based,
- transaction/take rate,
- license,
- rental/lease,
- service fee,
- outcome/performance based,
- advertising/sponsorship,
- data or insight products where lawful and appropriate,
- cross-subsidy/freemium,
- hybrid models.

At this stage, choose hypotheses and rough price/value logic. Detailed pricing optimization belongs in the Pricing & Monetization Playbook.

# 9.6 Key Resources

What must be owned, controlled or reliably accessed?

Examples:

- people/capabilities,
- technology,
- IP,
- brand/trust,
- proprietary data,
- distribution access,
- capital,
- licenses,
- supply,
- installed base.

Distinguish **must own** from **must access**.

# 9.7 Key Activities

What must the business do exceptionally well for the model to work?

Examples:

- product development,
- demand generation,
- sales,
- fulfillment,
- operations,
- risk assessment,
- matching,
- quality assurance,
- data curation,
- customer success,
- ecosystem governance.

Do not list generic company functions. List activities that are causally important to the model.

# 9.8 Key Partnerships

Who performs important activities or controls important resources?

Ask:

- Why does the partner participate?
- Who controls the customer relationship?
- Where is bargaining power?
- What happens if the partner changes terms?
- Could the partner integrate forward/backward?
- Is the partnership a moat or a dependency?

# 9.9 Cost Structure

What are the structurally important costs?

Separate:

- fixed vs variable,
- upfront vs ongoing,
- customer acquisition,
- implementation/onboarding,
- cost to serve,
- infrastructure/model/API usage,
- support,
- partner rev-share,
- working capital,
- compliance/risk costs.

The goal is not a detailed budget. It is to make the economic architecture visible.

# 10. Make the canvas a system, not nine boxes

Strategyzer’s own maturity framing distinguishes a beginner use of the canvas as a checklist from stronger models where components reinforce one another. Academic activity-system research reaches a similar conclusion: business models are systems of interdependent activities. [S16]

## 10.1 Write the business-model story

Every canvas should be explainable as a causal story:

```text
Because we target [segment] with [job/problem],
we offer [value proposition].
We reach and serve them through [channels/relationship].
They pay through [revenue mechanism].
To deliver this repeatedly we need [resources] and [activities],
with [partners] doing [roles].
This creates [cost structure],
which is economically plausible because [core economic logic].
```

If the story breaks, the canvas is not coherent.

## 10.2 Dependency map

Mark critical dependencies:

```text
Value Proposition -> requires Key Activity A
Key Activity A -> requires Partner B
Partner B -> takes 30% revenue share
Revenue share -> changes margin
Margin -> requires price > X
Price > X -> increases adoption assumption
```

This is where hidden business-model contradictions surface.

## 10.3 Activity-system lens

Zott and Amit recommend looking beyond individual components to the activity system. Their framework highlights:

- **Content** - which activities are performed?
- **Structure** - how are activities linked and sequenced?
- **Governance** - who performs and controls them?

They also discuss potential value-creation themes including novelty, lock-in, complementarities and efficiency. An earlier empirical study associated novelty-centered designs with performance in its specific sample, but the historical sample and design mean this should not be turned into a universal rule that "more novelty is better." [S16][S17]

Use this as a stress-test on the left side of the BMC and on ecosystem models.

## 10.4 Strategy and business model must fit

Research by Zott and Amit found evidence that business-model design and product-market strategy can be complements rather than substitutes. The implication for this playbook is simple: a model should not contradict the organization’s actual strategic positioning and capabilities without an explicit reason and transition plan. [S18]

## 10.5 Incentive and stakeholder-surplus check

For every actor whose cooperation is required, ask:

```text
WHAT VALUE DO THEY RECEIVE?
WHAT COST / RISK / WORK DO THEY BEAR?
WHAT CHOICE DO THEY CONTROL?
WHAT WOULD MAKE THEM DEFECT, BLOCK OR BYPASS THE MODEL?
```

A canvas can be internally tidy and still fail because one critical partner, employee group, channel, payer or platform has a weak or negative incentive to participate. Treat that mismatch as a design problem before validation.

## 10.6 Competitor and ecosystem response

Casadesus-Masanell and Ricart emphasize that a business model should not be evaluated only in isolation from other players. [S19] Stress-test:

- likely incumbent response,
- bundling/cross-subsidy by larger actors,
- supplier/channel repricing,
- platform rule changes,
- imitation of visible features,
- whether system-level complementarities remain harder to copy.

# 11. Use business-model patterns to expand the design space

Patterns are recurring configurations that can inspire alternatives. Strategyzer explicitly promotes business-model patterns as design inspiration rather than a focus only on product, technology or price. [S08]

## 11.1 Pattern library

Use this as a trigger list, not a taxonomy carved in stone.

### Transaction
Customer pays once for the product/service.

### Subscription
Customer pays recurring access fee.

### Usage-based
Customer pays based on consumption.

### Marketplace / take rate
Platform captures a fee on transactions between sides.

### Freemium / cross-subsidy
One user group or tier is free/low cost and economics are funded by another group/tier.

### Razor / consumables
Low-margin or subsidized initial product creates recurring complementary purchases.

### Licensing / IP
Customer/partner pays for right to use protected capability/content/technology.

### Product-as-a-service / rental
Customer buys access or outcome instead of ownership.

### Outcome-based
Payment tied to a measurable result. This requires careful alignment of control, measurement and risk; research on outcome-based models shows the importance of aligning value creation and value capture throughout the process. [S24]

### Managed service
Provider performs an ongoing process for the customer.

### Self-service / automation
Customer performs more of the work, often changing cost-to-serve and experience.

### Community / user contribution
Users contribute content, data, inventory, labor or knowledge that increases value.

### Ecosystem / platform complements
Third parties create complementary value around a core.

### Bundling
Multiple jobs/products combined into one purchase logic.

### Unbundling
Activities or offers separated to create specialization or different economics.

### Open / partner-leveraged model
External contributors or partners perform important innovation, production or distribution activities.

## 11.2 Pattern fit questions

For any pattern ask:

- What customer behavior must be true?
- What unit economics must be true?
- What capability or platform property must be true?
- What new risk does the pattern create?
- Which BMC blocks must change with it?
- What becomes easier?
- What becomes harder?

### Anti-pattern rule

Never add a subscription, marketplace, AI agent or community layer because the pattern is fashionable.

# 12. Generate alternatives in parallel

One of the most important design standards in this playbook is deliberate divergence before convergence.

A Stanford experiment in a design setting found that participants who produced alternatives in parallel created more diverse and higher-performing designs than participants iterating serially on one option. The evidence is from graphic-design prototyping, not business-model design, so it should be treated as supportive rather than direct proof. The underlying implication - reduce fixation by comparing alternatives - is strongly aligned with design practice. [S13]

## 12.1 Alternative-generation default

For a meaningful new business-design problem, **three materially different architectures is a strong practical default**, not an evidence-backed universal threshold. Use fewer only when hard constraints genuinely narrow the space; use more when the decision is highly consequential or the design space is broad.

Good alternatives vary at least two of:

- customer segment,
- job/outcome,
- value proposition mechanism,
- channel,
- relationship model,
- revenue model,
- operating model,
- partner structure,
- ownership vs access,
- scope/bundle,
- geographic/market entry logic.

## 12.2 Forced alternative prompts

1. **Customer shift:** What if the payer is a different actor?
2. **Channel shift:** What if we cannot sell directly?
3. **Ownership shift:** What if we rent/partner instead of own?
4. **Revenue shift:** What if pricing moves from seat to usage/outcome?
5. **Service shift:** What if the software becomes a managed service, or vice versa?
6. **Scope shift:** What if we solve only one high-value job extremely well?
7. **Platform shift:** What if we enable others rather than serve the customer ourselves?
8. **Cost shift:** What if customers/partners perform a key activity?
9. **Market shift:** What if we start with the narrowest high-value segment?
10. **Technology removal:** What if we must deliver the outcome without the novel technology?

## 12.3 Independent first, recombine second

Generate coherent alternatives independently before combining attractive pieces. Otherwise teams often create a "Frankenstein canvas" where locally appealing components have incompatible economics or incentives.

## 12.4 Search before you brainstorm only from memory

Use the external-search set from Section 3.2 before the final divergence round. Research on business-model innovation supports the relevance of external knowledge search, while the design literature supports widening alternatives before convergence. [S13][S32]

A useful sequence is:

```text
INTERNAL IDEAS
-> EXTERNAL / ANALOGOUS MODEL SEARCH
-> NEW ALTERNATIVES
-> COMPARE WHOLE SYSTEMS
-> RECOMBINE ONLY AFTER COHERENCE CHECK
```

# 13. First-principles viability check

Detailed financial modeling is downstream. Structural economic impossibility should not be.

## 13.1 Minimum economics

Estimate ranges, not false precision:

```text
Price / revenue per unit or customer
- direct variable cost
- partner/revenue share
- implementation/onboarding cost amortization
- ongoing cost-to-serve
= rough contribution before acquisition/overhead
```

Then ask:

- Is there room to acquire the customer?
- Does working capital break the model?
- Is human service intensity compatible with price?
- Does usage increase value faster than cost?
- Is infrastructure/API/model cost structurally bounded?
- Does the model require impossible retention or utilization?
- Who bears risk and when?

## 13.2 Earn-before-spend and other Strategyzer challenge questions

Strategyzer’s classic business-model assessment uses questions about switching costs, scalability, recurring revenues, earning before spending, getting others to perform work, protection from competition and game-changing cost structures. These are useful **challenge questions**, not universal scoring laws. [S06]

For example:

- Recurring revenue is valuable when value is recurring and retention economics work.
- High switching costs can improve retention but may create adoption friction, regulatory concerns or poor customer experience.
- Getting others to do work can reduce cost but may reduce quality/control or create unfair incentives.
- Scalability is valuable for many models but is not the objective of every viable business.

Use the questions to find design opportunities, not to optimize every model toward the same archetype.

# 14. Survivability, adaptability and responsible value

Strategyzer’s current 2026 assumption-mapping material uses **survivability** as the environment-facing risk category alongside desirability, feasibility and viability. Older Strategyzer material has also used **adaptability**. This playbook uses survivability as the primary risk label and adaptability as a design property within it. [S04]

## 14.1 Survivability questions

- What regulatory changes can kill the model?
- What platform dependency can kill the model?
- What supplier/partner dependency can kill the model?
- What competitor response can neutralize the advantage?
- What happens if the key technology becomes commoditized?
- What happens if the key input cost doubles or halves?
- Can the model survive a change in customer acquisition economics?
- Does the business depend on a temporary arbitrage?
- Does a powerful actor have an incentive to copy/integrate the model?

## 14.2 Adaptability questions

- Which components can change without redesigning the whole model?
- Which assets/capabilities are reusable across segments?
- What is reversible vs irreversible?
- Where can the business stage commitments?
- Which learning can be generated before fixed cost is locked in?

McGrath’s discovery-driven approach to business models emphasizes experimentation and evolutionary learning in uncertain contexts rather than assuming analysis alone can specify the final model. [S20]

## 14.3 Responsible-value check

IDEO explicitly frames human-centered design as not only asking what can be created, but what **should** be created; ISO innovation-management principles likewise center realization of value for interested parties. [S11][S41]

Before readiness, ask:

- Who could be harmed, excluded or made materially worse off if the model works as designed?
- Does the revenue model create incentives that conflict with customer/user welfare?
- Are safety, privacy, labor, environmental or social externalities material to the concept?
- Is trust a prerequisite that the operating model undermines?
- Would the team defend the model if its mechanics were transparent to customers, employees and regulators?

This is not a generic ESG scoring exercise. It is a **business-design integrity check** for foreseeable material externalities and perverse incentives.

# 15. Assess the concept without confusing design quality with market evidence

Strategyzer now provides a structured "Questions for Leaders" assessment that scores the strength of a Business Model Canvas across its building blocks and front-stage/back-stage/profit-formula patterns. That is useful **design diagnosis**. It is not evidence that customers will buy, that unit economics will hold in reality, or that the concept will win. [S38]

Use two parallel outputs:

1. **Design state** - is the concept coherent enough to test?
2. **Optional diagnostic score** - where does the team see relative design strength/weakness?

Never convert the second into a predicted probability of market success.

## 15.1 Design-state classification

For each dimension classify:

- `CLEAR` - design is coherent enough for the next phase.
- `UNCERTAIN` - design is plausible but depends on explicit assumptions.
- `BROKEN` - internal contradiction, hard constraint, responsibility issue or missing mechanism prevents meaningful testing without redesign.

## 15.2 Assessment dimensions

### A. Customer specificity
Can we identify exactly who the concept serves, in what context and with what job?

### B. Value relevance
Does the concept focus on a small number of meaningful jobs, pains and gains?

### C. Customer-perceived differentiation
Is the relevant difference clear against real alternatives?

### D. Business-model coherence
Do the nine BMC blocks reinforce rather than contradict one another?

### E. Value capture plausibility
Is there a credible reason the organization can capture enough value?

### F. Feasibility
Can required activities/resources/partners plausibly be assembled?

### G. Activity-system advantage
Is there a distinctive configuration, complementarity, efficiency, lock-in or other system-level advantage worth exploring? [S16]

### H. Environment/survivability
Does the model remain plausible under known regulatory, competitive and structural conditions?

### I. Incentive / responsibility integrity
Do critical stakeholders have a reason to participate, and are there no unresolved material harms or perverse incentives that make the design unacceptable?

### J. Strategic fit
Does the model fit the sponsoring organization’s strategic intent, or is the deliberate departure explicit?

### K. Testability
Can the critical assumptions be converted into specific validation questions?

## 15.3 Concept assessment card

```yaml
customer_specificity: CLEAR
value_relevance: UNCERTAIN
customer_differentiation: CLEAR
business_model_coherence: CLEAR
value_capture: UNCERTAIN
feasibility: CLEAR
activity_system_advantage: UNCERTAIN
survivability: CLEAR
incentive_responsibility_integrity: CLEAR
strategic_fit: CLEAR
testability: CLEAR

fatal_issues: []
critical_unknowns:
  - "willingness to pay at target economics"
  - "buyer willingness to grant system access"
```

## 15.4 Optional diagnostic score

If a team benefits from Strategyzer-style scoring, use a 1-5 rating **per design dimension or building block** to identify where redesign attention is needed. Always keep:

- the raw dimension ratings visible,
- the reasoning/evidence beside each rating,
- `BROKEN` issues separate from averages,
- market evidence separate from design opinion.

**Do not use a weighted total such as 82/100 as the go/no-go criterion.** A high internal score can coexist with zero demand; a low score can identify a design weakness without proving the opportunity is bad.

# 16. Extract and prioritize assumptions

Once a concept is coherent, convert it from "a story we believe" to "a set of claims reality can attack."

Strategyzer’s current Assumptions Mapping process extracts assumptions across desirability, feasibility, viability and survivability, then prioritizes high-importance, low-evidence hypotheses. [S04]

## 16.1 Ask the master question

> **What would have to be true for this concept to work?**

## 16.2 Desirability assumptions

Examples:

- The segment experiences the job/pain frequently enough.
- The problem is important enough to change behavior.
- The proposed outcome is valued.
- The switching/adoption friction is acceptable.
- The decision-maker recognizes the same value as the user.

## 16.3 Feasibility assumptions

- Required technology can achieve needed performance.
- Key integrations are possible.
- Required data can be accessed lawfully.
- Service quality can be delivered consistently.
- Key partners will participate.

## 16.4 Viability assumptions

- Price/value relationship works.
- Cost to serve is below allowable level.
- Channel economics work.
- Acquisition can be funded.
- Retention/repeat behavior is sufficient.
- Working capital is manageable.

## 16.5 Survivability assumptions

- Regulation allows the model.
- Platform dependency remains acceptable.
- Competitors cannot instantly neutralize the advantage.
- Key partner concentration is manageable.
- The model survives foreseeable market shifts.

## 16.6 Prioritization

Plot assumptions on:

```text
importance to success x current evidence strength
```

Prioritize assumptions that are:

- highly critical to the model,
- weakly evidenced,
- capable of killing or materially changing the architecture,
- tied to an irreversible or expensive commitment,
- practical to learn about before that commitment is made.

Do not force an arbitrary fixed number. In many projects **3-5 top assumptions** is a useful starting backlog; in regulated, multi-sided or capital-intensive concepts, more may be necessary. The Market Validation & Pretotyping Playbook determines the actual experiment sequence.

# 17. Concept Design readiness gate

The business-design phase is complete when the concept is **ready to learn from reality**.

## 17.1 READY TO VALIDATE

Use when all are true:

- Primary customer segment(s) are explicit.
- Important B2B stakeholder roles are separated where relevant.
- Customer Profile is focused and evidence-tagged.
- Value Proposition targets prioritized jobs/pains/gains.
- Real alternatives and customer-perceived differentiation are explicit.
- BMC is complete enough to explain creation, delivery and capture.
- No unresolved internal contradiction makes the model logically impossible.
- Rough economics are plausible enough to test.
- Environment, strategic interaction, hard constraints and responsible-value risks have been screened.
- Multiple materially different architectures were considered; three is the default for meaningful innovation work unless the design space is genuinely constrained.
- Key assumptions are explicit and prioritized.
- The team can state what the next validation tests need to learn.

## 17.2 REDESIGN

Use when:

- customer/value logic is vague,
- BMC blocks contradict each other,
- value capture is missing,
- stakeholder incentives do not align,
- economics fail on first principles but alternatives exist,
- a different segment/pattern/channel may resolve the issue,
- the concept cannot yet be made falsifiable.

## 17.3 PARK / DROP BEFORE VALIDATION

Only for a hard reason such as:

- legal/regulatory prohibition with no acceptable alternative,
- technical/physical impossibility with no plausible workaround,
- structurally impossible economics across reasonable design alternatives,
- unavailable critical resource/partner with no substitute,
- concept is outside strategic mandate and has no sponsor/path,
- a superior internal alternative makes further exploration irrational,
- repeated redesign still cannot produce a coherent, testable business logic.

### Important

**Lack of proof that customers want it is not a design-stage kill criterion.** That is exactly what market validation exists to discover.

# Part III - Deep design modules

# 19. New venture / startup module

Prioritize speed of concept exploration over organizational completeness.

Focus on:

- primary segment / plausible beachhead,
- bottom-up sense of whether the initial segment is worth learning about,
- acute job/problem,
- differentiated value mechanism,
- credible initial channel,
- price/revenue hypothesis,
- cheapest viable operating model,
- founder/unfair-access advantage,
- riskiest assumptions.

Do not spend the phase designing departments, org charts, detailed brand systems or five-year headcount. MIT's Disciplined Entrepreneurship curriculum is a useful complementary lens here because it explicitly sequences segmentation, beachhead selection, end-user profiles and bottom-up market analysis before broad scaling assumptions. [S42]

### Startup concept test

Can the founder explain:

```text
WHO has WHAT job/problem,
WHY the current alternative is insufficient,
WHAT we will offer,
WHY it creates meaningfully better value,
HOW we reach/deliver it,
HOW money flows,
WHAT must be true next.
```

If not, the concept is not ready for validation.

# 20. Existing product -> new market / segment module

Never assume an existing value proposition transfers intact.

Rebuild at least:

- Customer Profile for the new market,
- alternative/competitive set,
- buying process,
- channel assumptions,
- pricing/value logic,
- regulation,
- cost-to-serve,
- partner requirements,
- language/cultural context where relevant.

Then compare:

```text
CORE MODEL THAT CAN STAY
vs.
ELEMENTS THAT MUST LOCALIZE
vs.
ELEMENTS THAT MUST BE REDESIGNED
```

The question is not "Can we sell the Danish product in Germany?" but "What business model would allow this underlying capability/product to create and capture value in the German context?"

# 21. B2B / enterprise module

B2B concepts often fail on buying-system design rather than end-user value.

## 21.1 Map the whole decision unit

Create profiles for materially different roles:

- end user,
- manager/beneficiary,
- champion,
- economic buyer,
- IT/security,
- legal/compliance,
- procurement.

## 21.2 Separate value cases

```text
USER VALUE      = easier/faster/better work
MANAGER VALUE   = output, control, quality, capacity
ECONOMIC VALUE  = revenue, cost, risk, cash, headcount, throughput
IT VALUE        = secure, maintainable, integrable
PROCUREMENT     = comparable, contractable, low supplier risk
```

A strong enterprise proposition often needs several linked value propositions, not one slogan.

## 21.3 Implementation is part of the product

If adoption requires data access, integrations, migration, training or change management, those are part of the business model and economics.

# 22. Marketplace / platform module

## 22.1 Minimum platform canvas questions

For each side:

- Why join?
- Why stay?
- What creates liquidity/value?
- What are trust risks?
- What is the chicken-and-egg path?
- What is subsidized?
- Who pays?
- When does direct disintermediation become attractive?

Platform owner:

- matching,
- trust/safety,
- rules,
- payment,
- dispute resolution,
- data,
- discovery,
- quality control,
- economics.

Zott and Amit’s activity-system lens is particularly valuable because value is co-created across firm boundaries. [S16]

# 23. Service / consultancy / productized service module

Service businesses should explicitly design the relationship between customer value and human labor.

Ask:

- What is bespoke vs standardized?
- What is expert judgment vs repeatable process?
- What can be software-enabled or automated?
- What customer input is required?
- What creates quality variation?
- What determines utilization?
- Can pricing reflect value rather than hours?
- Which assets are created once and reused?

### Productization ladder

```text
BESPOKE SERVICE
-> STANDARD PROCESS
-> PRODUCTIZED SERVICE
-> SOFTWARE-ENABLED SERVICE
-> SELF-SERVICE / SOFTWARE
```

Do not assume moving right is always better. The optimal point depends on customer complexity, trust, willingness to self-serve and economics.

# 24. Physical / industrial product module

Add explicit design for:

- manufacturing,
- inventory,
- lead times,
- working capital,
- logistics,
- maintenance,
- warranty,
- installation,
- distribution margin,
- certifications,
- end-of-life,
- service/consumables opportunities.

A physical product can have a digital/service business model; do not let the artifact determine the revenue model by default.

# 25. Regulated / high-trust module

For finance, health, employment, safety-critical, public-sector or other regulated contexts:

- regulation is a design constraint,
- explainability/trust may be part of the value proposition,
- auditability may be a Key Activity/Resource,
- procurement and approval roles may be separate customer stakeholders,
- liability allocation changes partner and cost structure,
- speed cannot be optimized independently of safety/compliance.

Never leave legal feasibility until after the value proposition has been emotionally adopted by the team.

# 26. AI-native business design in 2026

AI changes the cost and speed of designing, delivering and operating business models. It does **not** remove the need for business design.

Strategyzer’s 2026 value-proposition guidance explicitly warns against confusing new technology with customer value, a point also echoed by recent strategy work on AI: technology should serve a value-creation logic rather than become the strategy itself. [S07][S30]

## 26.1 Good uses of AI in business design

- Summarize a provided evidence pack.
- Cluster interview and support themes.
- Generate alternative customer hypotheses.
- Generate multiple BMC/VPC variants quickly.
- Stress-test contradictions across canvas blocks.
- Generate counterfactual business-model patterns.
- Identify missing stakeholder roles.
- Draft scenario changes in the environment.
- Challenge unit-economics assumptions.
- Build comparison matrices.
- Turn a selected concept into a structured validation handoff.

## 26.2 Bad uses

- Invent synthetic customer evidence.
- Treat simulated personas as real research participants.
- Cite hallucinated market numbers or unverifiable sources.
- Pick the "best" canvas based on model preference.
- Generate customer quotes and label them interviews.
- Let generated summaries erase contradictory evidence or minority patterns.
- Use AI confidence as validation confidence.

## 26.3 AI evidence rule

```text
AI output without a real source        = ASSUMPTION or DESIGN OPTION
AI synthesis of a real source          = INFERENCE linked to source
AI extraction from a verified source   = SECONDARY_EVIDENCE or PRIMARY_DATA, preserving provenance
Real observed customer behavior        = PRIMARY_DATA
```

The human/team remains responsible for checking provenance, scope, contradictions and whether the source supports the exact claim.

## 26.4 AI-enabled economics

Because AI can reduce the cost of software creation, content, analysis and some service delivery, old assumptions about what must be standardized or automated may change. Revisit:

- minimum viable scale,
- service/software boundary,
- cost-to-serve,
- personalization economics,
- gross margin,
- new competitors enabled by lower entry barriers.

AI lowering build cost can also make imitation easier, which may shift advantage away from features toward data, distribution, trust, workflow integration, activity-system fit or ecosystem position.

# 27. Corporate innovation / existing organization module

Existing companies face a different design problem from startups: the new model must coexist with existing assets, incentives and businesses.

Strategyzer’s portfolio work distinguishes exploration of new business models from exploitation of existing proven models. [S10]

Ask:

- Which existing assets genuinely advantage the concept?
- Which existing incentives or processes constrain it?
- Does the new model cannibalize the old one?
- Does it need a separate brand/channel/team?
- Is there a transfer point from exploration to operating business?
- Who owns the P&L before business-model fit exists?
- Which corporate constraints are real vs inherited habit?

Research on organizational design and business model innovation also emphasizes the importance of fit/misfit between business-model choices and organizational structures. [S23]

# Part IV - Decision science for business design

# 28. Separate four kinds of decisions

## 28.1 Facts about the world

Example: "German employment law requires X."

These should be verified.

## 28.2 Inferences about the world

Example: "Because customers currently use consultants for this task, trust may matter more than self-service."

These should show the evidence and uncertainty.

## 28.3 Design choices

Example: "We will initially target finance teams with 20-200 employees."

These are strategic decisions, not discoveries.

## 28.4 Assumptions to validate

Example: "At least enough finance teams will pay for an AI operator to support the target economics."

These belong in the validation backlog.

A strong team does not argue about all four categories in the same way.

---

# 29. Use reversible and irreversible choices differently

Under uncertainty:

- Keep irreversible commitments low until assumptions have stronger evidence.
- Make reversible design choices quickly when they are cheap to change.
- Preserve multiple options when the evidence does not justify convergence.
- Identify decisions that create path dependency in technology, partnerships, brand, contracts or capital structure.

This is consistent with discovery-driven business-model thinking: uncertain models should evolve through learning rather than premature commitment. [S20]

---

# 30. Concept portfolio logic

If several concepts compete for resources, compare them on:

- strategic relevance,
- potential value,
- customer importance,
- differentiated mechanism,
- ability to access the market,
- critical constraints,
- evidence already available,
- cost/time to learn,
- reversibility,
- portfolio overlap.

Do not pretend you can precisely forecast which early idea will win. The decision is which concepts deserve the next unit of learning investment.

Recent business-model research also suggests that external knowledge search can support business-model innovation, reinforcing the value of looking beyond internal assumptions when generating alternatives. [S32]

# Part V - Contradiction ledger

# 31. "The Business Model Canvas is the business model"

**Status:** Too literal.

The canvas is a representation. The real model is the operating logic and activity system. [S16][S21]

**Rule:** Use the canvas to reason about the model, then inspect real activities, incentives and economics.

# 32. "The Business Model Canvas is strategy"

**Status:** Incomplete.

Business model and strategy overlap but are distinct constructs. Research suggests they can complement one another. [S18][S19]

**Rule:** Keep a separate strategic choice layer: where to play, how to compete, which capabilities/positions matter.

# 33. "A Value Proposition Canvas proves product-market fit"

**Status:** False.

Strategyzer itself says fit is a claim until customers confirm it. [S02]

**Rule:** VPC is a design and hypothesis tool; Market Validation provides evidence.

# 34. "Customer interviews validate demand"

**Status:** Usually false.

Interviews can strongly improve understanding of context, jobs and pains, but stated future purchase is weaker evidence than behavior/commitment.

**Rule:** Use interviews for discovery; use the Market Validation playbook for behavioral evidence.

# 35. "Jobs-to-be-done replaces segmentation"

**Status:** Too categorical.

JTBD is useful for understanding customer progress and context. Hard constraints and economically relevant attributes can still matter.

**Rule:** Segment on the variables that change value, behavior or business-model economics.

# 36. "Design thinking is a fixed five-step process"

**Status:** Misleading.

Stanford d.school explicitly emphasizes abilities over one universal recipe; IDEO describes an iterative approach rather than a deterministic sequence. [S11][S12]

**Rule:** Use the operating loop flexibly while preserving the underlying disciplines.

# 37. "Always start with a customer problem"

**Status:** Too narrow.

Innovations can start from technology, regulation, assets or business-model shifts.

**Rule:** Whatever the origin, translate it into a customer/stakeholder value hypothesis and coherent business model.

# 38. "More features create more value"

**Status:** Often wrong.

Strategyzer emphasizes focus on the jobs, pains and gains that matter most and meaningful differentiation rather than maximum coverage. [S07]

**Rule:** Optimize value relevance, not feature count.

# 39. "Recurring revenue is always a better business model"

**Status:** False as a universal rule.

Subscription is attractive only when recurring value, retention, billing logic and customer preference support it.

**Rule:** Choose revenue logic to fit value delivery and behavior.

# 40. "High switching costs are always good"

**Status:** False as a universal rule.

Switching costs can improve retention but increase adoption friction and can conflict with trust, customer value or regulation.

**Rule:** Prefer earned retention over artificial captivity where possible.

# 41. "A platform is more scalable, therefore better"

**Status:** False.

Platforms create coordination, liquidity, governance and chicken-and-egg challenges.

**Rule:** Use platform models only when cross-side interactions create real additional value.

# 42. "A five-year forecast proves viability"

**Status:** False.

A forecast can quantify assumptions but cannot validate unknown demand or economics.

**Rule:** Use first-principles ranges in business design; detailed modeling follows stronger evidence.

# 43. "The concept with the highest internal score wins"

**Status:** False precision.

No universal design score predicts market success.

**Rule:** Use structured review to expose weaknesses, then let evidence decide critical uncertainties.

# 44. "We need to finish the concept before showing anyone"

**Status:** Dangerous.

Human-centered design and discovery-driven approaches rely on iterative learning. [S11][S20]

**Rule:** Keep artifacts low resolution until the question requires higher resolution.

# 45. "If we cannot prove demand yet, we should kill it"

**Status:** Wrong phase logic.

Business design prepares a concept for validation.

**Rule:** Kill at this stage only for a hard design/constraint/portfolio reason. Missing demand evidence becomes a validation task.

# Part VI - Anti-playbook

# 46. Practices to actively avoid

1. Filling the BMC alone in a meeting and calling it customer-centric.
2. Writing the product features before defining the customer job.
3. Combining multiple customer segments into one generic persona.
4. Putting solution features in the Customer Profile.
5. Treating all pains/gains as equally important.
6. Defining value as "AI-powered", "innovative", "seamless" or "platform" without a customer outcome.
7. Treating a named competitor list as the full alternative set.
8. Copying the dominant category business model without understanding why it works.
9. Choosing subscription because investors like recurring revenue.
10. Choosing marketplace because two groups exist.
11. Adding partners without defining partner incentives.
12. Ignoring the actor who actually controls budget.
13. Ignoring implementation and adoption cost in B2B.
14. Treating Key Activities as a generic department list.
15. Treating Key Resources as a shopping list.
16. Treating Customer Relationships as "good service".
17. Treating Channels only as advertising channels.
18. Building one canvas and polishing it for weeks.
19. Generating many canvases that differ only cosmetically.
20. Combining the best-looking blocks from alternatives without checking coherence.
21. Using TAM size to compensate for weak customer value.
22. Using a five-year Excel forecast to hide uncertain unit economics.
23. Calling internal executive opinion "market evidence".
24. Calling AI-generated personas "customer research".
25. Treating interview compliments as purchase intent.
26. Treating a framework creator’s case story as universal causal evidence.
27. Ignoring regulation until after concept selection.
28. Ignoring competitor response and ecosystem power.
29. Optimizing every Strategyzer assessment question simultaneously.
30. Treating an internal canvas/design score as evidence of demand or as a predicted success probability.
31. Ignoring foreseeable negative externalities or stakeholder incentives because they sit outside the nine canvas boxes.
32. Searching only direct competitors and missing analogous business models elsewhere.
33. Moving to validation without a prioritized list of critical assumptions.

# Part VIII - Worked examples

# 59. Example A - AI finance operator for SMEs

This is a **design example**, not evidence that the concept is attractive.

## Frame

Opportunity: AI systems can perform increasingly complex information-work workflows. SMEs often have fragmented finance operations without capacity for a large finance team.

## Customer architecture

Primary user: finance manager/controller.  
Economic buyer: CFO/CEO.  
Approvers: IT/security/accountant depending on setup.

## Customer Profile hypothesis

Jobs:

- close the month accurately,
- prepare management reporting,
- reconcile data across systems,
- explain anomalies,
- maintain audit trail.

Pains:

- repetitive collection/copying,
- late inputs,
- inconsistent spreadsheets,
- dependence on one employee,
- little time for analysis.

Gains:

- earlier close,
- fewer manual handoffs,
- traceability,
- reliable process,
- more time for decision support.

All are assumptions until connected to real evidence.

## Value proposition A - software assistant

Software coordinates data gathering, reconciliation suggestions and reporting package, while finance retains approvals.

Revenue: subscription + implementation.  
Key risk: willingness to integrate sensitive finance data.

## Value proposition B - managed AI finance service

Provider delivers defined monthly finance outputs using software + human review.

Revenue: monthly service fee.  
Key risk: labor/service cost and responsibility.

## Value proposition C - accountant channel product

Sell the system to accounting firms so they can deliver more capacity to many SME clients.

Revenue: platform/license/usage.  
Key risk: partner incentive and channel control.

The important business-design learning is that one underlying technology can support **three materially different business models**. The correct next step is not feature prioritization; it is comparing the business architectures and validating the critical assumptions.

---

# 60. Example B - Danish consumer product entering Germany

## Bad framing

> "How do we market our Danish product in Germany?"

This presumes the existing concept transfers unchanged and jumps toward GTM.

## Better business-design framing

> "What customer, value proposition and business model would allow the underlying product/capability to create and capture value in Germany?"

## Redesign questions

- Is the same customer job important?
- Are pains/gains different?
- Which alternatives dominate?
- Is the price/value relationship equivalent?
- Does the current channel work?
- Is a distributor required?
- Do packaging, certification or claims change?
- Does retailer margin change economics?
- Is the brand credible without Danish context?
- Should the bundle change?

Output: a Germany-specific VPC/BMC plus explicit list of what can remain unchanged and what must be validated.

---

# 61. Example C - Two-sided expert marketplace

## Side A: businesses

Job: get specialized help quickly.  
Pain: supplier search and contracting.  
Gain: trusted capability on demand.

## Side B: experts

Job: access relevant paid work.  
Pain: inconsistent pipeline and admin.  
Gain: qualified demand and easy contracting.

## Platform value

Matching + trust + contracting + payment + dispute handling.

## Critical business-model questions

- Which side is harder to acquire?
- Which side should be subsidized?
- Can the platform create liquidity in a narrow segment first?
- How is quality verified?
- Why do both sides stay on-platform after matching?
- What take rate is compatible with off-platform alternatives?
- Who owns liability?

A one-sided canvas would hide the actual system.

# Part IX - One-page Golden Standard

# 62. Concept & Business Design - one page

1. **Frame the business-design question before opening a canvas.**
2. **Use Strategyzer BMC + VPC as the shared backbone, not as proof or a rigid process.**
3. **Bring existing evidence into the room and label what each source actually supports.**
4. **Search beyond direct competitors: substitutes, analogues, adjacent models and credible failures.**
5. **Map the environment: market, industry, trends, macro, regulation and material societal constraints.**
6. **Translate external forces into explicit business-model implications and assumptions.**
7. **Map the full customer/stakeholder ecosystem before selecting a primary design focus.**
8. **Create one Customer Profile per materially distinct segment/role where jobs and value differ.**
9. **Map functional, social and emotional jobs plus pains and gains, grounded in context.**
10. **Prioritize the few customer jobs/pains/gains that matter most and tag their evidence status.**
11. **Use structured interviews/observation to refine customer understanding, not to claim demand validation.**
12. **Make value concrete with Value Scenes before polishing proposition language.**
13. **Design a focused Value Map with explicit pain-relief/gain mechanisms.**
14. **Use value-proposition patterns to widen options, never as guaranteed recipes.**
15. **Differentiate on dimensions customers actually care about and include real alternatives such as manual work and doing nothing.**
16. **Design the whole business model: value creation, delivery and capture.**
17. **Make channel, relationship, revenue, activities, resources, governance and partner incentives deliberate choices.**
18. **Inspect the activity system for dependencies, complementarities, bottlenecks and system-level advantage.**
19. **Stress-test how competitors, platforms, suppliers and partners can respond if the model works.**
20. **Generate multiple materially different concept architectures before convergence; three is a practical default, not a law.**
21. **Use business-model patterns and external analogues to expand the design space.**
22. **Run rough economic plausibility before detailed forecasts.**
23. **Stress-test survivability, reversibility, regulation, trust and responsible value.**
24. **Use structured design assessments diagnostically; never treat an internal score as market evidence.**
25. **Tag every important claim as evidence, inference, assumption, design choice, constraint or unknown.**
26. **AI may generate options, synthesize and extract from real sources; it may not invent evidence.**
27. **Extract assumptions across desirability, feasibility, viability and survivability.**
28. **Prioritize critical low-evidence assumptions, especially before expensive or irreversible commitments.**
29. **Finish with a one-page Concept Definition, selected BMC/VPC, evidence register and uncertainty map.**
30. **Move into Market Validation when the concept is coherent and falsifiable - not when the team feels certain.**

# Source-handling rules

1. **ISO standards define broad innovation-management boundaries; they do not prove one canvas or sequence is universally best.**
2. **Strategyzer is the operating backbone, not a claim of scientific supremacy.**
3. **Academic research is used to challenge and deepen the framework, not to decorate it.**
4. **Case examples do not become universal rules.**
5. **Historical platform/company examples are illustrative, not current implementation guidance.**
6. **A framework recommendation is labeled as a framework recommendation, not a causal finding.**
7. **A customer or internal data point is evidence only for the claim it actually measures.**
8. **No fixed success percentage, number of alternatives, or canvas/design score is treated as universal without robust evidence.**
9. **Concept-design evidence must not be misrepresented as downstream market validation.**
10. **AI-generated content is never elevated above its real source; provenance must survive synthesis.**
11. **When sources conflict, preserve the conflict and convert context-dependent claims into design questions.**
12. **Use the newest authoritative source for current terminology, but preserve older concepts when they remain useful and label the difference.**

# Final doctrine

> **A great business concept is not the cleverest idea, the fullest canvas or the most polished pitch. It is a focused hypothesis about how a specific customer will receive meaningful value from a coherent system that can plausibly create, deliver and capture value - with every important uncertainty made explicit enough to test.**

The operating discipline is:

```text
UNDERSTAND THE CONTEXT
-> DEFINE WHO MATTERS
-> UNDERSTAND THE JOBS / PAINS / GAINS
-> DESIGN FOCUSED VALUE
-> DESIGN THE WHOLE BUSINESS SYSTEM
-> CREATE REAL ALTERNATIVES
-> STRESS-TEST COHERENCE + ECONOMICS + ENVIRONMENT
-> LABEL EVIDENCE VS ASSUMPTIONS
-> SELECT A TESTABLE CONCEPT
-> VALIDATE IT IN THE REAL WORLD
```

The Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas provide the shared language. ISO's front-end innovation guidance confirms the broader discipline while explicitly rejecting a one-method-fits-all assumption. Human-centered design supplies the discovery and exploration mindset. Business-model research adds system, strategy, governance, interaction and value-capture depth. Current Strategyzer methods add customer ecosystems, value scenes, structured assessment and assumption mapping. The Market Validation & Pretotyping Playbook then takes over where this playbook intentionally stops: **reality decides whether the designed concept deserves further investment.**
