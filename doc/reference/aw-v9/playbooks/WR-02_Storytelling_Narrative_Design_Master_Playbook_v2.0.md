## Executive standard

A story is fundamentally **a representation of something that happens**. In the broad narratological sense, even one event can count as narrative; scholarship disagrees about whether two events or a causal connection are necessary for the category. In *practical narrative design*, a useful story usually makes it possible to understand **who or what was affected, what changed or was challenged, how events were connected, and why the change matters**. A causal, goal-directed design is a strong *default for explanatory and strategic narratives*, not an ontological test that every authentic story must pass. [N-ABB][N-ZWAAN][N-ARIST]

A narrative is **not** automatically the best representation of a message. Lists, evidence summaries, direct instructions, factual explanations, timelines and tables may be better when the reader needs a fast answer, an accurate comparison, a procedure or a full account of uncertainty. Stories have documented advantages in some comprehension, memory and persuasive settings; direct matched comparisons and population-level replications show important limitations and heterogeneity. [N-MAR21][N-RAH25][N-STAT22][N-HET21]

### Governing doctrine

> **Find the truthful change worth understanding. Give it only the people, context, causality, tension, detail and interpretation it has earned. Make the past explain the present, and the future emerge as a possibility rather than a promised ending. Use a story only when a story improves the audience's real task.**

The operating chain:

```text
AUDIENCE / PURPOSE / CONTEXT
→ STORY OR NON-STORY DECISION
→ FACTS / SOURCES / BOUNDARIES / CONSENT
→ SUBJECT / INITIAL SITUATION / EVENT(S)
→ INTENTIONS / FORCES / CONSEQUENCES
→ CAUSAL / TEMPORAL / THEMATIC THROUGHLINE
→ WHAT CHANGES + WHY IT MATTERS
→ POINT OF VIEW / VOICE / DETAIL
→ REVEAL / SCENE / EXPLANATION / PACING
→ HONEST ENDING / UNRESOLVED QUESTIONS
→ CLAIM + MEANING + ETHICS REVIEW
→ AUDIENCE EVALUATION WHERE WARRANTED
→ CONTROLLED PUBLICATION + LEARNING
```

This is a **reasoning loop**, not a mandatory linear drafting process. A narrative can legitimately be quiet, fragmented, collective, nonchronological, comic, unresolved or structurally unconventional. The conditions it must satisfy depend on its intended job and truth-status.

### The seven protected invariants

1. **An event is not the same thing as a claim about its cause.** Chronology never independently proves causation.
2. **A compelling structure is not permission to invent a fact.** Real people, testimony, numbers, quotations and outcomes remain constrained by evidence.
3. **Emotional truth is not a substitute for factual truth.** A narrator's genuine feeling can coexist with disputed recollection or incomplete knowledge.
4. **Narrative causality must be supportable.** `After this` may be known when `because of this` is not.
5. **People are not props.** Do not reduce a customer, employee, vulnerable person or community to a device for a brand's heroism.
6. **Narrative usefulness must be earned.** `NO_STORY` and `SHORT_ANECDOTE_ONLY` are positive, permissible decisions.
7. **Existing successful prose should be preserved.** Diagnose before rewriting; preserve before improving; every material change needs a real reader, truth, structure or ethics problem [P-WR00].

### Fast decision rule

```text
IF the reader needs a direct fact, instruction, specification, evidence table or time-critical action:
    Start with that form. Story is optional supporting context, never the obstacle.
ELSE IF actual events and their consequences can help the reader understand a meaningful change:
    Consider a story; identify the narrative and its evidence before drafting.
IF meaningful structure requires invented crisis, private emotion, conflict, victory or dialogue:
    Do not invent it. Simplify, label fiction/hypothesis, or choose non-story.
IF the audience could infer an unsupported cause or generalization from an individual case:
    Add context, disclose limitations, or avoid the illustrative case.
IF a finished narrative does not improve the intended outcome over a simpler alternative:
    Prefer the alternative.
```

## 1.3 Intended uses, counteruses and rigor

Typical uses: explaining how an organization changed direction, portraying a real customer's experience, connecting an individual's work over time, describing the origins of a business, making strategic trade-offs imaginable, or providing a memorable but truthful example in editorial explanation.

Not suitable as a default: urgent safety instructions, privacy notices requiring precise terms, release notes, pricing tables, configuration instructions, contract provisions, direct search answers, troubleshooting runbooks or highly technical comparison tasks. Such content **may** contain a short illustration, but its required information must remain easily accessible.

| Risk/impact | Minimum posture |
|---|---|
| Low (personal note, low-stakes illustration) | Source/meaning check and ordinary editorial review. |
| Material (public founder story, customer case, strategy narrative) | Evidence ledger, consent/privacy check where appropriate, causal audit, stakeholder review, content-status labeling. |
| High (health, finance, safety, legally sensitive accusations, vulnerable subjects, high-impact public policy) | Independent competent domain review, stronger source corroboration, explicit uncertainty and approval rights; relevant external standards/law take precedence. |

**Rigor is a function of consequence, not production value.** A glossy campaign can be low-risk; a short first-person testimonial about an individual's medical outcome can be high-risk.

# 2. Research architecture and evidence discipline

## 2.1 Research question and design

**Question.** Which narrative properties and creation/verification practices are most defensible across real personal, editorial and organizational communication, which effects are context-dependent, and which popular story formulas are descriptive craft aids or unsupported performance claims?

**Design.** Targeted multidisciplinary scoping synthesis, primary theory/source checks, peer-reviewed empirical synthesis, direct contradictions, practitioner-method provenance, current editorial/ethics review, author-side scenarios and falsification. Not a formal systematic review: searches were targeted rather than exhaustive; study screening/extraction was not performed by independent reviewers; no pooled effect estimate was calculated in this document.

**Search period/cutoff:** current web-source checks on **10 October 2026** plus the uploaded canonical playbooks. Search families: `define narrative event causality narratology Abbott Aristotle`, `event indexing intentionality causal inference narrative`, `story memory expository meta-analysis`, `narrative versus nonnarrative matched persuasion meta`, `transportation counterarguing replication heterogeneity`, `narrative identity coherence longitudinal`, `corporate narrative strategy change research`, `story circle hero journey model criticism`, `journalistic reconstruction quotation consent trauma`, `AI writing story diversity homogenization`, `future scenario not prediction`. Publisher and professional-body pages were preferred for published metadata and claims; paywalled sources are used within the bounds of available official abstract/method descriptions. [N-ABB][N-MAR21][N-RAH25][N-REUTERS]

**Selection logic:** use scholarship for definitions and mechanisms; empirical syntheses for effects; institutional ethical codes for their own field practices; official original practitioner material for descriptions of frameworks, never for proving their universal effectiveness; internal approved company evidence for a company's actual stories. Exclude invented effect sizes, vague neuroscience marketing, unsupported 22× memory claims and unverifiable testimonials.

## 2.2 Claim classes, confidence and requirement words

MPS classes apply: `REQ` (externally binding in scope), `EST` (established), `DEF` (recommended default), `CTX` (context-dependent), `EMG` (emerging), `HOUSE` (chosen house rule), `EXP` (hypothesis), `UNK` (unresolved). `MUST/MUST NOT` are **house requirements** unless explicitly scoped to an external authority. `SHOULD` is a strong contextual default; `MAY` is optional. The force of a recommendation is separate from confidence in a causal effect. [P-MPS]

**Research lanes:** formal/narratological analysis is strong for concepts but does not determine the best conversion message; meta-analyses test average effects and heterogeneity, not your precise website; a news ethics code governs its practitioners and supplies useful integrity controls elsewhere without pretending it is global law; a story guru owns their technique's definition but cannot prove it boosts revenue by reputation.

`HIGH` = strong fit, independent corroboration or scoped formal requirement. `MODERATE` = credible but domain-/design-limited. `LOW` = limited, contested or indirect. `INSUFFICIENT` = no defensible conclusion. Confidence belongs to **specific claims**; none of these labels constitutes a universal narrative-quality score.

## 2.3 Evidence summary and its tensions

| Specific question | Strong finding | Contrary/bounding evidence | Operational disposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What minimally defines narrative?** | Abbott: representation of event(s); scholars disagree about minimum count/causality. [N-ABB] | A classical plotted tragedy prioritizes coherent action and order. [N-ARIST] | Minimal definition broad; consequential explanatory story prefers causal/goal coherence (`EST/CTX`). |
| **Does narrative aid memory/comprehension?** | Mar et al. (2021): >33,000 people, 150 effects; mean `g≈0.55` narrative advantage, high heterogeneity. [N-MAR21] | Genre/content/task matching and publication differences restrict transport to particular application. | Useful prior for learning/recall tasks, not guaranteed individual or business outcome (`EST/CTX`). |
| **Does story always persuade more than direct communication?** | 2020 synthesis found immediate and delayed advantages in its experimental sample. [N-LONG20] | Rahmani et al. (2025): sizable effects vs control, but narrative versus information-equivalent nonnarrative differences far less conclusive. Narrative-vs-statistical evidence meta-analysis found near-zero overall difference. [N-RAH25][N-STAT22] | Do not mandate story; test equivalent-information alternative (`CTX`). |
| **Does transportation mediate persuasion?** | Green & Brock experiments, van Laer synthesis identify absorption and links with story-consistent judgments. [N-GREEN][N-VANLAER] | Xu et al. found opposite transportation effects for identical message across samples; story can increase counterarguing. [N-HET21] | Treat mechanism and audience interaction as conditional, not guaranteed (`CTX`). |
| **Are meaningful life stories always beneficial?** | Narrative-identity research associates some coherence constructs with functioning and meaning. [N-MCADAMS][N-WATERS15] | A larger young-adult study only partially confirmed well-being predictions; samples/outcomes differ. [N-POEL19] | Never demand redemption, therapy or trauma disclosure in public biography (`CTX`). |
| **Does a fixed structure describe every story?** | Aristotle and modern practitioner systems provide powerful construction lenses. [N-ARIST][F-MCKEE][F-HARMON] | Modern narratology recognizes many event/nonlinear/experimental forms; Campbell universalism is disputed. [N-ABB][F-CAMP-CRIT] | Frameworks optional diagnostic scaffolds, not necessary laws (`CTX`). |
| **Can AI write useful stories?** | Doshi & Hauser (2024) found benefits to judged individual short-story creativity. [N-AICREATE] | Same experiment found lower between-story variety; 2026 meta-analysis finds modest task-dependent homogenization. [N-AIHOMO] | AI can assist structure; never use fluency as provenance or force generic arc (`HOUSE/CTX`). |

**Critical distinction:** `narrative vs no message`, `narrative vs unrelated fact message` and `narrative vs information-equivalent explanation` are **different experimental comparisons**. Likewise comprehension, delayed recall, emotional absorption, belief, action and ethical appropriateness are different outcome variables. A claim about one is not proof of another.

## 2.4 Author-side contradiction protocol

Before elevating a narrative rule to `EST`, attempt to break it with **at least**: (a) a credible contrary research result, (b) a non-Western/collective/experimental or understated story, (c) a high-consequence real-person scenario, (d) a direct-explanation comparator, (e) a different reader-state and (f) an AI that follows the rule mechanically while violating the intent. The audit and dispositions appear in §22. Disagreement is retained where the underlying estimand, genre or study population differs.

## 2.6 Evidence-to-recommendation logic for WR-02

- **`EST` / HIGH within a named scope:** preservation of event/source integrity; reader needs and context; existence of memory/comprehension effects in studied text comparisons; distinguish causality from temporal sequence. `EST` does **not** mean any particular story will be successful.
- **`DEF` / MODERATE:** choose a clear throughline, source-supported specifics, meaningful progression and proportionate ending for explanatory nonfiction. These are reasoned writing defaults triangulating theory and practice, not controlled proofs of a single ideal format.
- **`CTX` / task-dependent:** three acts, scene-first openings, emotional arc, tension, first person, flashbacks, transportation, social-identification mechanisms and narrative-led persuasion.
- **`HOUSE` / mandatory-in-this-standard:** source/causal ledger, no invented nonfiction detail, reviewer-identified audience benefit before major rewrite, truth-mode labeling, proportional person/privacy controls, AI semantic diff and correction route.
- **`EMG / EXP / UNK`:** optimal model/prompt for AI voice preservation, multicultural genre performance, organization-wide effects of a change narrative, whether narrative produces business effects against an equally informative comparator in a particular audience.

**Evidence-to-decision test.** A publication choice may be strong because the harm of getting it wrong is serious even if no RCT tests a particular editorial safeguard. Conversely, a statistically positive average transportation effect is *not* a mandate to use stories when they are irrelevant or ethically costly. `MUST` expresses this house rule or a specifically identified external obligation; it does not itself prove causal effectiveness. # 3. The domain model: when facts become a story

## 3.1 Minimal narrative, useful story and effective story are separate categories

**Minimal narrative (`NARRATIVE_MIN`)**: an account representing at least one occurrence/event and some entity involved. Descriptive sentences about a static condition do not meet this minimal event distinction; a single reported action can. Some influential theorists demand two or more events or a causal relationship, while others do not. **There is no universally agreed strict boundary** [N-ABB].

**Developed narrative (`NARRATIVE_DEVELOPED`)**: a selection and ordering of events and entities that makes meaningful development interpretable. It may contain goals, conflicts, recurrence, thematic resonance, uncertainty or a changing situation, but none requires sensational drama.

**Purposeful narrative (`NARRATIVE_PURPOSEFUL`)**: a developed narrative that earns its communication role by helping its actual audience understand an experience, relation, decision, change, risk or value better than an acceptable non-story alternative.

**Well-verified real-world narrative (`NARRATIVE_TRUSTWORTHY`)**: a narrative whose material facts, attribution, chronology, causal implications, first-person voice, private information, representativeness and significant omissions survive a risk-proportionate audit. Emotional impact cannot repair truth failure.

## 3.2 The essential distinction: events vs. timeline vs. causal narrative

An **inventory** names facts: “The company was founded in 2019; it hired employees in 2021; it entered a new market in 2023.”

A **timeline** orders events: “First the company was founded; then it hired; later it expanded.” Chronology is useful and may already be a simple narrative, but chronological proximity does not explain motivation, mechanism or consequence.

A **causal account** states supportable links: “After customer support requests rose, the team introduced an internal knowledge base. Interviews with the employees involved indicate that repeated questions were one reason for that decision.” It distinguishes what happened from what the evidence supports about why.

A **developed organizational story** adds situation, persons or affected groups, a decision under constraints, meaningful consequences and a revisable interpretation. It must not turn one interview into an unqualified causal evaluation of a company-wide intervention.

**Causality ladder (`HOUSE`):**

| Expression | What must be supported |
|---|---|
| `A happened, then B.` | Reliable ordering only. |
| `After A, the team decided to do B.` | Ordering + documented decision. |
| `The team reported A as a reason for B.` | Authentic, fairly represented testimony; not necessarily objective causation. |
| `A contributed to B.` | Evidence for a causal contribution, not merely temporal coincidence. |
| `A caused B.` | Stronger causal evidence, assessment of alternative explanations, scope. |
| `Without A, B would not have happened.` | Counterfactual evidence substantially stronger; rarely justified by anecdotes alone. |

Do not replace missing evidence with smoother transitions like “therefore,” “so,” “which inevitably led to” or a dramatic montage.

## 3.4 Primary narrative components

- **Subject/character:** who or what carries the experienced change? A person, team, community, institution, product, ecosystem or even an implicit focal point. The subject need not be a lone hero.
- **Situation:** the relevant initial condition and environment, not the subject's entire backstory.
- **Intention/desire:** what the actor was trying to achieve, *when supported*. Non-agentic causes and accidental events do not require invented desires.
- **Disruption/change:** an event alters expectations, conditions, knowledge or relationships. Small changes count.
- **Obstacle/tension:** uncertainty, competing aims, trade-offs, friction or unresolved consequence. There need not be an enemy or catastrophic setback.
- **Stakes:** why the outcome matters to the people concerned, at the actual scale of consequence.
- **Progression:** each selected event contributes information, consequence, deepening complication, change or resolution; pure connective padding can be removed.
- **Resolution/state:** what is actually known at the end. Open endings and partial progress are valid.
- **Premise:** the organizing proposition/question that makes event selection coherent.
- **Theme:** the interpretation or recurring concern invited by the events, not a compulsory final moral.
- **Throughline:** the stable question/change that links beginning, middle and ending despite shifts in time or viewpoint.
- **Narrator/voice:** who tells the account, what they know, when and how; identity and authorization may matter.
- **Discourse:** the *telling* (selection, order, viewpoint, detail, pace), distinct from the underlying events [N-ABB][N-GENETTE].

## 3.5 The Narrative Truth–Form Matrix (V2)

A *story's world* and a *story's telling* are different objects. The event record can contain verified happenings, credible memories, unresolved claims, disputed statements and modeled future possibilities. The published representation additionally chooses viewpoint, order, emphasis, emotional intensity and silence. An account can therefore fabricate nothing and still mislead through selection or arrangement.

| Layer | Editor asks | Protected constraint |
|---|---|---|
| **World/event** | What happened, to whom, where/when, and with which documentary basis? | No unsupported event, person or quotation is upgraded into observed fact. |
| **Source** | Which direct record, witness, memory, inference or third-party report supports it? | One witness's recollection is attributed unless corroborated; source conflict stays visible when material. |
| **Links** | Is A before B, plausibly linked, demonstrated cause, or speaker's theory? | Sequence or narrative plausibility is not causal identification. |
| **Discourse** | What is placed first, omitted, compressed, juxtaposed or emphasized? | Editing must not generate a material false implication. |
| **Narrator** | What does this voice legitimately know or claim? | No omniscient access to private motive or inner thought without source. |
| **Time horizon** | Is a statement past fact, current observation, intention, forecast or scenario? | Possible future is not achieved fact. |
| **Audience** | What does a reasonable reader infer *after* the story? | Implied meaning is part of the truth gate. |

**Example (synthetic teaching case).** Documents show a team introduced a new process in January, while response times improved in March. A causal account may correctly say *the improvement followed the introduction*; it must not say *the process caused the improvement* absent evidence that accounts for changing staffing, mix, demand, measurement and concurrent process changes. If a manager attributes improvement to the process, label it as their assessment. A real A/B test or defensible causal design permits a more specific statement within its estimand. [N-ZWAAN][P-MEAS]

## 3.6 Agency, structural conditions and multivoice

- **Subject** means what or whom the account follows; it need not be a single protagonist. A company story can track a team, changing organizational practice, product system or affected community.
- **Agent** means an actor whose choices are evidenced as consequential; agency can be bounded by material constraints and collective decisions. Do not use narrative simplicity to assign all credit to the CEO/founder.
- **Opposition** is not synonymous with villain. Constraints, uncertainty, disagreement, cost, incompatibility and conflicting legitimate goals may provide meaningful tension without demonizing an actor.
- **Multiple perspectives** are necessary where a single voice would misstate a disputed or collective outcome. Do not conflate fairness with giving scientifically unsupported claims equal evidential status.
- **Unresolved outcomes** are not editorial failure. Explain what happened, what remains uncertain and what the audience should not infer.

Organizational research treats stories as interpretive resources that can stabilize *or* challenge institutions. A polished official story that silences employees' differing experiences is not automatically a successful change narrative. [N-AOM16][N-AOM24]

## 3.7 Three separate criteria: existence, quality and use

`IS_NARRATIVE`: the artifact represents at least an event under a declared truth/fiction mode, allowing scholarly disagreements on minimum event count. `COHERENT_FOR_JOB`: a reader can follow the relevant event(s), transitions, context and sufficient relations without unsupported leaps. `PREFERRED_FOR_JOB`: it improves a reader-relevant outcome over an adequate alternative at acceptable time/ethical cost. A candidate can pass the first criterion and fail the second or third. Never treat a narrative-schema match as a quality score. [N-ABB][N-ABB2020][P-MPS]

# 4. Narrative quality, assurance and success model

Judge by **fit**, not a one-number “story score.” An apparently poetic, emotional or dramatic piece can fail as a truthful company history, a useful founder bio or a reliable customer case.

| Dimension | Core reviewer question | Distinct failure |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose / choice of form | Does story outperform a concise direct alternative for this reader job? | Story tax: user must read drama to find basic information. |
| Truth / provenance | Could every material factual implication be defended? | Invented scene, quote, motive or causal chain. |
| Event legibility | Can the reader identify what happened and to whom? | Abstract philosophy with no occurrence. |
| Causal coherence | Does the explanation distinguish cause, sequence and speculation? | Post hoc inference disguised as fact. |
| Relevance / selection | Are scenes, turns and details useful for the governing question? | Backstory overload or decorative side quests. |
| Character / agency | Are involved people represented proportionately? | Company takes credit for customer effort. |
| Stakes / proportion | Does significance follow actual consequence? | The minor inconvenience becomes a fabricated crisis. |
| Perspective / attribution | Is narrator knowledge and location honest? | Omniscient claims about private feelings. |
| Theme / meaning | Does interpretation emerge plausibly from evidence? | Unearned lesson and preachy epilogue. |
| Form / progression | Does order reveal necessary understanding without confusing the reader? | Time jumps for mere novelty. |
| Closure / uncertainty | Is the actual outcome or open state represented fairly? | False redemption or promised transformation. |
| Ethical treatment | Do dignity, privacy, agency and power imbalances receive review? | Trauma mining or invisible third-party harm. |
| Comprehension / memory | Can representative readers retell what occurred and its limits? | Readers only remember mood or quote, not reality. |
| Organizational fit | Does the story align with authorized brand reality without becoming propaganda? | Brand invents values through anecdote. |

A concise narrative can score well despite lacking a classic climactic arc. A reflective vignette can be excellent without a dramatic resolution. A strategic story can be strong while acknowledging genuine risk and a contested future.

### Rules deliberately *not* created in V2

There is **no** mandated number of beats, scene length, emotional ups/downs, first-person quotes, hooks, story timespan, adversity level, audience tears or conversion-rate lift. There is no evidence-backed universal percentage of readers who prefer narrative. The proper number of story components is whatever is necessary to preserve real event meaning and satisfy the particular reader's job at acceptable cost. [N-ABB][N-RAH25]

# 6. Decision architecture — story, explanation, evidence, or another form?

**Decision principle:** choose the communicative form that best supports the reader's job with the least distortion and necessary effort. A narrative is one option, not the default proof of sophistication. The cost of a story includes time, attention, evidentiary uncertainty, permission burdens, risk of distortion and future maintenance.

## 6.1 The Story-or-Not Decision

| Reader's primary job | Default delivery | When narrative earns a place | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Find an answer quickly | Direct answer; short explanation | Brief example after the answer | Withholding the answer for a dramatic hook |
| Follow a procedure or handle an incident | Ordered steps, warnings and stop conditions | Optional incident illustration separately | Turning safety-critical steps into suspense |
| Compare alternatives | Comparable facts, criteria, table | Case vignette to show lived consequences | A vivid but atypical case displacing representative evidence |
| Understand why something changed | Causal explanation + evidence | Event-led account if sequence and decisions matter | Calling mere succession causation |
| Understand a person | Factual biography + selected episodes | Scene or coherent personal account that reveals choices | Inventing a defining trauma or heroic transformation |
| Understand a company | Direct description + evidence | Origin/turning-point story when materially informative | Manufactured founder myth or customer-as-prop |
| Understand a customer's experience | Case facts, problem, intervention, result | Specific path of decisions, constraints and outcomes | Confusing one success with typical performance |
| Understand organizational change | Decision, rationale, consequences, action | Past → present → possible future with clear uncertainties | Pretending future adoption is predetermined |
| Consider a possible future | Explicit scenario and assumptions | Credible narrative to make contingencies tangible | Presenting a hypothetical as a prediction |
| Reflect, remember, connect or entertain | Personal narrative or artful account | Narrative is often the primary form | Forcing practical advice or a moral onto an open story |

### Executable branch logic

```text
1. Name ONE primary communication outcome and audience.
2. Ask whether the reader needs an answer, decision, instruction, proof,
   interpretation, identification, imagination or emotional connection.
3. If direct information satisfies the job, choose NO_STORY unless a
   demonstrable secondary need requires narrative.
4. If narrative may help, identify real event(s), what changed, stakes,
   and what the account can and cannot claim.
5. If these cannot be established without invention, choose EXPLANATION,
   FACTUAL_TIMELINE, PROFILES or HYPOTHETICAL_LABELED_SCENARIO instead.
6. Test expected benefits against length, context, truth, privacy and
   maintenance costs.
7. Choose FULL_NARRATIVE, MICRO_NARRATIVE, EXPLANATION_WITH_ANECDOTE,
   DIRECT_EXPLANATION, EVIDENCE_SUMMARY, PROCEDURE or NO_PUBLICATION.
8. Reassess when the subject, evidence or reader's job changes.
```

## 6.2 Directness and disclosure outrank dramatic structure

A safety alert, pricing disclosure, cancellation instruction, factual correction, regulatory explanation or urgent operational update MUST NOT hide material information until a story's ending. A story may accompany the necessary direct message only if it does not obscure timing, scope or action. This is a `HOUSE` risk control rooted in truthfulness and usability, not a claimed universal experimental law.

## 6.3 No-story is a successful output

A valid WR-02 deliverable can be: “Use a direct three-sentence explanation. There is no event arc relevant to the reader's decision.” An AI system MUST be able to recommend no narrative rather than manufacturing a story to satisfy its own genre bias.

---

## 6.3 V2: Decision matrix for narrative necessity and information cost

**Diagnostic before draft** — answer these in order:

1. *What question must the person answer after reading?* If it can be answered by a fact, boundary, process or concise comparison, **direct explanation wins by default**. A story may still provide an optional example after the essential answer.
2. *Do particular events matter to that answer?* A narrative earns a place when lived sequence, choice, cause, identity, uncertainty or observed consequence adds decision-relevant meaning.
3. *Can we source the selected events, their links and the implied claim?* If not, seek more evidence, narrow the account, make it hypothetical with an explicit label, or stop.
4. *What will the reader lose?* Weigh word/time cost, discoverability, avoidable emotional burden, omitted facts, confusion about typicality and stale proof risk.
5. *Would a mixed format outperform pure narrative?* Common answer: direct claim → short sourced story → explanation/evidence → limitations/next step. There is no shame in exposition.
6. *Is the intended outcome measurable?* For material scale decisions, compare against an equally accurate, comparably polished, appropriately short non-story version rather than a weak straw control.

| Conditions | Choose | Why | Release blocker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urgent instruction, hazard, support repair or specific fact | `DIRECT` or `PROCEDURE` | Readers should not hunt for the actionable answer | Suspense hiding necessary action |
| Account of a complex series without established causal link | `FACTUAL_TIMELINE` plus explicit uncertainty | Preserves chronology without invented causal arc | Post hoc causality claimed as fact |
| A meaningful decision or shift with reliable participants, constraints and observed results | `FULL_NARRATIVE` or `CASE_STORY` | Sequence and human agency materially explain change | Unverified central event, result or motive |
| A fact needs a concrete example but the reader primarily needs explanation | `EXPLANATION_PLUS_BRIEF_ANECDOTE` | Uses lived detail without delaying the answer | Anecdote falsely presented as representative data |
| Unfamiliar future possibilities and trade-offs | `LABELED_SCENARIO` plus assumptions and alternatives | Makes possible pathways legible without false certainty | Scenario reads as prediction, commitment or achieved result |
| Disputed experience and community-level consequences | `MULTIVOICE_ACCOUNT` with source status | Keeps material interpretations and agency visible | Suppresses material disagreement or fabricates consensus |
| No truthful event, no reader benefit, or privacy harm outweighs gain | `NO_STORY` / `DO_NOT_PUBLISH` | Refusal to over-narrate is a successful design choice | Manufactured crisis or unauthorized disclosure |

### The counterfactual editing question

> *If every sentence remained literally accurate but the sequence, scene choice and omissions were different, would a reasonable reader come away with a different view of responsibility, frequency, cause or success?* If yes, review the **whole implied account** before publication. [N-REUTERS][N-NIEMANCLARK04]

**Stop condition:** a required plot beat has no source, the brand owner demands unsupported claims, or the narrative implies a guaranteed personal/financial/health outcome. Stop and route to the accountable owner. Narrative craft does not establish authority to override truth or legal constraints. [P-WR01][P-BRAND]

This is a **house template**; it does not mean every personal anecdote needs a database. Use the lightest traceability that can defend the consequential claims. For a private, low-stakes memory, source tags can be in ordinary notes; for a published investor/customer/employee case, preserve a structured chain of custody.

## 7.2 Fact-status taxonomy

- `VERIFIED_RECORD`: contemporaneous document, dataset, recording or other independently checkable support. Its meaning can still be ambiguous.
- `CORROBORATED_ACCOUNT`: at least two meaningfully independent consistent sources, with agreement limits visible.
- `ATTRIBUTED_TESTIMONY`: a person's reported recollection or interpretation; truthful attribution does not prove external accuracy.
- `UNCORROBORATED_RECOLLECTION`: remembered account with uncertain precision; never silently turn it into a transcript.
- `EDITORIAL_INFERENCE`: an explicit interpretation of events; alternatives should be considered where material.
- `UNKNOWN`: unresolved fact, date, motive, outcome or causal link; MUST NOT be fabricated.
- `ILLUSTRATIVE_FICTION`: invented teaching example; visibly identified as fictional where confusion is possible.
- `HYPOTHETICAL_SCENARIO`: internally coherent possible course of events; assumptions and possibility status disclosed.
- `COMPOSITE`: details merged from multiple subjects/situations; disclosure and permissions as applicable, never represented as a single identifiable true case.

The `VERIFIED_RECORD` label refers to verification of the *record and its bounded content*, not omniscience about what truly occurred. For instance, an email sent at 14:03 supports that the message was sent then; it cannot prove what every recipient understood or why they acted.

## 7.3 Questions to ask before drafting

**For the event:** What exactly happened? Who witnessed it? What can we date? What was already true beforehand? What changed afterward? Which plausible factors besides the apparent trigger could explain the change? Where is the record incomplete?

**For the people:** Who has standing to tell this story? Whose voice is used or missing? What are the subjects' own interpretations? What permissions or redactions are warranted? Does the publication create avoidable consequences for employees, customers, family members or vulnerable people?

**For the reader:** What might the reader falsely assume? Is this case typical? Does it represent a person or organization fairly? Would a non-story form make the information more accurately understood?

## 7.4 Interview practice

Prefer open, event-based prompts over leading narrative prompts. “What happened next?” is a neutral beginning; “What was the moment you knew you had conquered your fear?” plants a triumphant arc. Ask for the exact sequence, contemporaneous context, contradictory details, uncertainty and what the participant does *not* remember. Preserve the interviewee's language, subject to agreed attribution and quotation rules. Retrospective accounts may change across interviews; inconsistency is a diagnostic question, not automatic proof of deception. [N-OHA][N-MCADAMS]

## 7.5 Source-selection and survivor bias

A story whose evidence consists only of a satisfied customer's testimony does not justify a prevalence claim. Record why that case was selected and whether unsuccessful, ordinary or opposing cases would materially change the implied conclusion. If the story is chosen as an illustration of one possibility, SAY that; if used to imply a standard outcome, obtain representative evidence or remove the implication. [N-PNAS14][P-WR01]

## 7.6 V2 evidence-sufficiency test by content form

| Proposed element | Ordinary personal recollection | Commissioned customer case | Independent reported nonfiction | Future scenario |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Events and chronology | Speaker attribution plus documentary checks when high stakes | Source record + permission + material time boundaries | Independent corroboration proportionate to allegation | Clearly hypothetical assumptions |
| Direct dialogue | Exact audio/notes where available; distinguish recreated memory | Verify source and quotation authorization | Exact attributable sourced quotation only | Invented dialogue explicitly labeled as imagined |
| Inner thoughts/motive | Speaker may describe *own recollected* state; don't certify others' | Quote/attribute participant's account; no mind reading | Attribute to a source or documented record; otherwise omit | May use imagined representative viewpoints when labeled |
| Causal assertion | Distinguish felt explanation from demonstrated mechanism | Specify observed versus claimed commercial effect and alternative explanations | Stronger evidentiary basis where material; reflect disputes | Conditional links under explicit assumptions |
| Naming and consent | Respect affected parties, privacy and truth; speaker owns only their perspective | Contract/rights/permission and organizational authority | Ethical reporting and applicable law; no generic source veto | Do not imply imagined people are real |
| Typicality/generalisation | Personal experience should not become universal law | Compare with actual distribution where any typical-results implication exists | Contextualize unusual case and base rates | Not evidence of likelihood |

If evidence is incomplete, the valid actions are `SOURCE_MORE`, `ATTRIBUTE`, `QUALIFY`, `CUT`, `ANONYMIZE_WITH_INTEGRITY`, `LABEL_AS_HYPOTHETICAL`, `CHOOSE_DIRECT`, or `HOLD`. `INVENT` is not a repair path for factual nonfiction. Anonymization does not license silent changes to facts that materially distort the event; a composite can be a clearly declared illustrative construct but is not a particular witnessed person. [N-NIEMANCLARK04][N-OHA][P-WR01]

## 7.7 The unanswered-questions log

For every material narrative, record: decisive events that still lack sources; missing or conflicting timelines; who else may be affected; plausible rival explanations; what source/speaker may be unavailable; what claim changes most if new evidence emerges; what would prevent publication. These items are not draft defects to smooth away. They define the limits of what may be told. A source cannot supply their own independent corroboration merely by repeating an assertion. [P-MPS][N-REUTERS]

# 8. Subject, intention, obstacles, conflict and stakes

## 8.1 Subject and character are not synonymous with “hero”

A narrative subject can be a person, couple, community, team, institution, product, process, research project or place. A subject is narratively useful when relevant events can be tracked as affecting it. The subject need not be sympathetic, heroic, autonomous or emotionally articulate.

**Character model (contextual, not mandatory):**

```text
SUBJECT → ORIGINAL SITUATION → RELEVANT GOAL OR NEED
→ CONSTRAINT / EVENT → DECISION OR RESPONSE
→ CONSEQUENCE → WHAT WE NOW KNOW
```

For a tornado, scientific discovery or economic event, “goal” may be absent or collective; forcing an anthropomorphic desire would misrepresent the causal structure.

## 8.2 Intention, desire and need

Distinguish **expressed goals** (“I wanted to leave”) from **inferred goals** (“She must have longed for freedom”). Do not attribute unspoken private motivations as reported facts. If intent is relevant and knowable, preserve it; otherwise describe observed action and source-qualified interpretations.

## 8.3 Disruption, obstacle and conflict

A disruption is a relevant change in the initial state. An obstacle blocks or complicates a desired outcome. Conflict can be interpersonal, internal, social, material, informational or situational. These are **narrative possibilities**, not universal required stages. The tension of an honest case can be unresolved uncertainty or a real trade-off without a villain or dramatic failure.

| Source reality | Acceptable narrative tension | Unacceptable manufactured tension |
|---|---|---|
| Competing project priorities | Time and resource trade-off | Invented feud between colleagues |
| Slow company formation | Limited capacity and uncertainty | Fake “near-bankruptcy” crisis |
| Product not yet proven | Unknown adoption outcome | Claiming the market rejected it |
| Ordinary career shift | Reconsidered priorities | Invented personal trauma |
| Crisis with no resolution | Continuing consequences | Mandatory triumphant ending |

## 8.4 Stakes must be true and proportional

Stakes answer “Why does the outcome matter?” They can be modest: saving an hour, getting the first honest customer response, learning an unfamiliar skill. Quantify only when supported, and do not imply severe danger to inflate a mundane event. Larger-sounding stakes are not intrinsically better. Maintain a direct link from stakes to the reader's purpose.

## 8.5 Agency without hero worship

A narrative may center a founder, leader or customer while still crediting collaborators, background conditions, luck, institutional support and structural constraints. A protagonist is an **attention model**, not a license to erase other causes. Ask who actually did the work and who bore the cost.

# 9. Chronology, causality and the past–present–future throughline

## 9.1 Three distinct graphs

**The event timeline** answers *what happened and when*; **the causal map** proposes *how events influenced one another*; **the narrative throughline** selects *which relationships matter to the reader's question*.

```text
TIMELINE:         2021 A → 2022 B → 2023 C
CAUSAL MAP:       A --supported influence--> C
                  X --plausible alternative-> C
                  B --temporal, no proven link-> C
NARRATIVE LINE:   A mattered because it changed how C became possible;
                  B supplied context; X remains a genuine alternative.
```

Do not draw a causal arrow solely because the dates line up. Event-indexing research supports why coherent time, protagonist, causality and intentionality relationships aid readers' models; it does not prove every individual story must display each dimension as a formal step. [N-ZWAAN][N-CAUSAL]

## 9.2 Causal language ladder

| Evidence position | Safer formulation | Claim to avoid without stronger evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Only chronology known | “After the workshop, the team changed its process.” | “The workshop caused the improvement.” |
| Participant interpretation | “The manager credited the workshop with changing her approach.” | “The workshop transformed the company.” |
| Plausible mechanism, weak isolation | “The new process may have helped by reducing handoffs.” | “The process generated 40% growth.” |
| Documented specific mechanism | “The team removed two handoffs, allowing decisions without the former approval step.” | Extrapolating to unrelated outcomes |
| Credible causal evaluation | “In the evaluated setting, the change increased [specified outcome] by [estimated amount].” | Claiming permanent universal impact |

For material causal business/performance claims, hand off to WR-01's substantiation controls and the relevant measurement/experiment standard. Narrative coherence is not causal identification.

## 9.3 Past → present → future

A strong cross-time narrative need not be a chronological lecture. Often it has this logical structure:

```text
PAST — what context, experience or decision created today's constraints/capabilities?
PRESENT — what is demonstrably true, different or unresolved now?
FUTURE — what options, aspirations or conditional paths follow from those facts?
```

The *same throughline* should survive all three sections. For example, if the recurring question is “How do we make specialist knowledge less dependent on individual employees?”, the past describes an actual dependency, the present describes verified working responses, and the future describes an explicit aspiration with assumptions. Do not bolt a general inspirational vision onto an unrelated biography.

### The Future Truth Boundary

`OBSERVED_PAST` and `CURRENT_VERIFIED_STATE` are factual claims. `TARGET`, `COMMITMENT`, `PLAN`, `FORECAST`, `SCENARIO` and `ASPIRATION` are different types of future statement. The type must be knowable to the reader whenever it changes the interpretation. “We will launch in November” may be an organizational commitment; “By November everyone will benefit” is a prediction requiring a different evidence burden. [N-SHELL]

## 9.4 Flashbacks and nonlinearity

Chronology and disclosure order are independent choices. A narrative can open in the present, return to a formative event and move forward. A nonlinear arrangement SHOULD improve insight, suspense or emotional precision enough to justify orientation costs. A reader still needs temporal anchors when ambiguous timing could alter causality or responsibility.

## 9.5 Open endings

Unresolved is not unfinished when the unresolved state is the truth. Real change stories often end with “The process is in place, but we do not yet know whether it will scale.” Forcing closure falsely upgrades a trial to a transformation.

---

## 9.6 V2 causal-edge ladder and false-causality safeguards

Represent material transitions using the most defensible of the following terms:

```text
TEMPORAL:        A happened; later B happened.
ASSOCIATIVE:     A and B changed together in observed data.
ATTRIBUTED:      A participant says A contributed to B.
MECHANISTIC:     Records explain a plausible pathway from A to B.
CAUSAL_SCOPED:   A stronger design/analysis supports A's contribution to B
                 under stated population, assumptions and uncertainty.
UNKNOWN:         The relation remains unestablished or contested.
```

These labels are a **house provenance language**, not a standardized causal-inference theorem. Mechanistic plausibility is not automatically adequate proof of a claimed effect. `Because`, `as a result`, `solved` and `led to` imply different degrees of causal assurance depending on context. Review each consequential use. Include alternative changes in staffing, product conditions, competition, selection, confounding and measurement when they could reverse a reader's understanding. [P-MEAS][N-ZWAAN]

**Example, synthetic**

- Wrong: “A founder's new pricing model saved the company.” Evidence only shows that pricing changed in April and revenue rose in June.
- Supported chronology: “The pricing model changed in April. Revenue grew in June, alongside a new sales partnership.”
- Attributed interpretation: “The founder credits the pricing change with part of the turnaround; the partnership was also a factor.”
- Supported causal estimate: use only if a relevant experiment or defensible analysis can establish a bounded effect, and state its assumptions and interval where material.

### Explicit past–present–future contract

| Position | Truth status | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Past | Observable event or attributed memory | “We piloted two approaches in 2025.” |
| Present | Current verified condition and unresolved constraint | “The service now supports twelve participating teams.” |
| Future *commitment* | Accountable intention with owners/conditions | “We plan to expand after the security review.” |
| Future *scenario* | Conditional, illustrative possibility | “If adoption doubles, support capacity may become a bottleneck.” |
| Future *forecast* | Model output with assumptions and uncertainty | “The forecast projects a range, not a certainty.” |
| Future *vision* | Desired state distinct from promise | “We want more people to receive timely support.” |

Temporal coherence means the reader can follow **what changed, what remains and what is still possible**—not that history points toward an inevitable triumph. Shell explicitly separates scenarios from predictions, forecasts and its own strategy; that is operational provenance for the distinction, not a universal scenario framework. [N-SHELL26]

# 10. Premise, theme, meaning and throughline

## 10.1 Distinguish the concepts

- **Premise (working definition):** the central situation or proposition the narrative explores. It may be a question rather than a thesis.
- **Throughline:** the recurring causal, conceptual or human thread that allows the reader to connect episodes.
- **Theme:** a pattern of meaning the account explores—such as belonging, trust, scarcity, work, uncertainty or responsibility.
- **Message:** a conclusion the writer wants the audience to understand. In nonfiction, it remains constrained by evidence.
- **Moral:** a general normative lesson; optional and often unnecessary.

These are **craft working terms**, not standardized scientific constructs with one universally accepted boundary. Different narratology and writing traditions use them differently.

## 10.2 The throughline integrity test

Ask at every significant episode:

1. Does it change, complicate or illuminate the central question?
2. Does the audience need it to understand the consequences?
3. Does it connect to a preceding or later event without invented explanation?
4. If removed, would a material part of understanding, emotion or evidence be lost?

Remove or relocate an episode if the answer is no to all four—unless the narrative's legitimate aesthetic aim calls for texture beyond instrumental efficiency. Do not confuse a business explainer's editing criteria with artistic constraints on literary nonfiction.

## 10.3 Meaning should emerge from evidence

Meaning is a reasonable interpretation of the events, not automatically a “lesson learned.” A story can show a question becoming more complicated. Stronger reflective conclusions distinguish “I learned...” (personal interpretation) from “The company proved...” (external claim). Avoid appending “And that's why resilience is everything” when the evidence supports only one small adjustment.

## 10.4 Contrasts and reversals

A before/after or expectation/reality contrast is useful when it explains an actual change. Avoid stylized binary claims such as “We used to chase growth; now we create meaning” unless the record supports the contrast. Reality often combines continuities and discontinuities; preserve both. A surprise is not an editorial requirement.

# 11. Narrator, perspective, voice, scene and exposition

## 11.1 Point-of-view selection

| Perspective | Best legitimate use | Main risk | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| First-person (“I”) | Authored experience, carefully approved as-told-to account | AI ghostwriting invents inner life | Speaker review and voice-preservation diff |
| Third-person close | Reported biography with well-sourced perspective | Illicit access to private thoughts | Attribute thoughts to testimony/source |
| Multiple perspectives | Disputed/shared events, organizational change | False equivalence or confusion | Identify speaker, role, scope of knowledge |
| Institutional “we” | Verified collective action and accountable public commitments | Erases disagreement or credits one organization with others' work | Specify decision authority and contributors |
| Omniscient literary narrator | Fiction or openly literary construction | In nonfiction, appears to know impossible details | Constrain to available evidence or disclose fictionalization |

## 11.2 Voice is evidence-sensitive

In a founder story, a distinctive unpolished sentence can be stronger and truer than a polished corporate slogan. An AI draft SHOULD preserve source language, register, humor, cadence and genuine ambiguity where those are not barriers to comprehension. Do not imitate a person's inner monologue from third-party material. If ghostwritten, obtain review commensurate with the speaker's attributed authorship. [P-WR00][N-OHA]

## 11.3 Scene versus exposition

**Scene** places the reader in an identifiable event: who is there, what happens, what can be seen or heard, what changes. **Exposition/summary** compresses facts, patterns and background across time. Both are valid. Use scene for moments whose observable sequence and decisions matter; use summary for information whose significance does not require moment-by-moment reconstruction.

**Scenes in reported nonfiction are discovered, not manufactured.** Every quoted line, sensory detail, gesture, time, place or interior thought must have a legitimate source. “It was raining” requires evidence if attributed to a real day; “her heart raced” needs testimony or medically/physiologically appropriate support, not aesthetic instinct. [N-NIEMAN][N-REUTERS]

## 11.4 Show and tell are complementary

“Show, don't tell” is craft shorthand, not a universal rule. An exact statement—“Three partners declined”—can carry more meaning than a staged boardroom scene. Showing may improve involvement where the detail is material and sourced; telling can make causality, uncertainty and timelines legible. Use the combination that improves reader understanding and proportionality.

## 11.5 Pacing and revelation

Pace comes from the relationship between event density, time covered, reader expectations and paragraph-level selection, not prescribed chapter or sentence lengths. Defer a fact only when the reader can tolerate delayed disclosure. In commercial, health, financial or urgent contexts, a reveal that withholds essential qualifications becomes misleading. [N-GENETTE][P-WR01]

## 11.6 Openings and endings

**Opening jobs:** orient to a relevant person/event/question, establish the stakes proportionately, and let readers know what kind of account they are receiving. A direct first sentence is often best: “We spent six months learning why customers didn't use the feature.” A cinematic cold open is optional.

**Ending jobs:** state the real consequence, acknowledge remaining uncertainty, return to the meaningful question where useful, or leave a resonant unresolved detail. A future commitment may follow, but the story does not owe the reader a heroic victory or sales CTA.

# 12. Emotional movement, transportation and its limits

## 12.1 What the evidence justifies

Narrative transportation is a studied condition in which readers or viewers become mentally engaged with a story world. Research associates it with some shifts in beliefs and attitudes, and synthesized studies find context-dependent effects. It is **not** a guarantee that storytelling produces better comprehension, conversion or durable change, nor is emotional intensity a reliable proxy for truth. [N-GREEN][N-VANLAER][N-RAH25][N-HET21]

The 2021 narrative-versus-expository meta-analysis found an average narrative advantage for comprehension and memory in its collected study conditions, with substantial variation across contexts. A different synthesis directly comparing narrative and statistical evidence in persuasion found **no statistically reliable average advantage for either** across its sample of studies. These results are not contradictory once the dependent variables and comparison conditions are separated: *memory* is not the same construct as *belief or behavior change*. [N-MAR21][N-STAT22]

An individual story can create a vivid impression of typicality that is unsupported by base rates. The stronger the emotional anecdote, the more important it may be to show denominators, competing experiences or the selection mechanism when communicating scientific, health, social or commercial risks. [N-PNAS14]

## 12.2 Honest emotional arc

```text
INITIAL FEELING OR CONCERN (documented or speaker-owned)
→ EVENT / DISCOVERY
→ REACTION (documented, attributed or explicitly inferred)
→ SHIFT / NON-SHIFT
→ REFLECTION OR OPEN QUESTION
```

Not every story has a mood reversal. Fear can remain fear; satisfaction can grow without initial suffering. A speaker might not have had any dramatic feelings. Do not invent fear to create stakes or joy to manufacture resolution.

## 12.3 Ethical transport safeguards

1. Separate emotional engagement from factual comprehension in review.
2. Do not substitute a testimonial for evidence about frequency or efficacy.
3. Disclose financially relevant relationships and material case-selection limits according to applicable specialist/legal rules.
4. Avoid exploiting grief, trauma, illness or insecurity for attention where the communicative value does not justify the harm.
5. Give real people agency over material private disclosures where appropriate, without misrepresenting the publisher's independence or applicable journalistic norms.
6. Check likely reader inferences, not merely literal sentence accuracy.

---

## 12.4 V2 reader-state moderators and unintended narrative effects

Transportation is a plausible psychological process, not a quality certificate. The 2024 Thomas–Grigsby synthesis of 95 studies describes varied antecedents, outcomes and methods; even among quantitative studies, some used transportation theory without measuring the construct. Green & Appel's 2024 interdisciplinary review places misinformation, self/identity and correction alongside persuasion. Effects are contingent on readers, messages, conditions and comparators. [N-THOMAS24][N-GREENAPPEL24]

**Test at least five different reader consequences when material:**

1. *Understanding:* can readers accurately explain what happened and what remains uncertain?
2. *Inference:* do they incorrectly attribute causality, typicality, authority or completed success?
3. *Emotional proportionality:* does the emotional response reflect the situation without fear, shame or manipulation?
4. *Agency:* do readers know they may disagree, verify, refuse or take another path?
5. *Behavior and durability:* what choices persist, and is the causal contribution of the narrative actually evaluable?

**Repetition is a hazard, not only a branding mechanism.** Experimental work on the illusory-truth effect shows that repetition can increase perceived accuracy and downstream sharing of false statements; a large preregistered systematic map finds many studies rely on Western convenience samples and artificial trivia tasks. It would be an overreach to assert that *narrative* caused those specific repetition effects. The warranted applied precaution is to avoid repeating an unsupported claim across story, caption, excerpt and campaign until it gains an undeserved aura of truth. [N-ILLUS23][N-ILLUSMAP21]

### Ethics when emotion is the mechanism

A deliberately emotional story should still expose material trade-offs and uncertainty at the decision point, never make access to services or belonging contingent on confession, and never frame dissent as moral inferiority. Personal accounts and change stories are not therapy, consent substitutes or managerial coercion tools. [N-GANZ-ETH26][N-MCADAMS-BOOK]

# 13. Nonfiction truth, ethics, consent and representation

## 13.1 The Nonfiction Truth Contract

For public nonfiction, editorial teams MUST preserve all of these distinctions:

| Distinction | Required practice | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Observed vs reconstructed | A reconstruction must have verifiable sources and material uncertainty visible | “According to contemporaneous notes...” |
| Quote vs paraphrase | Direct quotations accurately represent recorded speech; edited paraphrases are not placed in quotation marks | Do not “improve” a person's sentence then quote it as exact |
| Recollection vs historical certainty | Attribute memory to the speaker when independently uncertain | “She remembers arriving before dawn” |
| Sequence vs causality | Use causal claims only when supported | “The sales decline followed the change” ≠ “The change caused the decline” |
| One case vs population | Disclose non-representativeness where material | One customer outcome cannot establish median savings |
| Real vs composite | Disclose composites when a real single case would be implied | “Composite illustration based on multiple interviews” |
| Observed vs imagined future | Mark scenarios and assumptions | “One possible 2030 outcome, not a forecast” |
| Event vs motive | Attribute subjective intention | “He said he hoped...” rather than “He secretly wanted...” |
| Real-world consequence vs narrative closure | Do not convert an open trial into a completed success | “The pilot is ongoing” |

An “emotionally accurate” scene with invented dialogue is still fabricated as nonfiction. If the author chooses imaginative reconstruction, change the declared genre and disclose it unambiguously when the reader could mistake it for reporting. [N-REUTERS][N-SPJ][N-NIEMAN]

## 13.2 Consent, privacy and editorial independence

No universal rule makes **subject approval** legally necessary or editorially desirable for every piece of reporting. Journalistic independence can be undermined by source veto; a private company biography, an oral-history interview, a testimonial campaign and investigative journalism have different legal and ethical duties. Select the applicable consent, privacy, attribution and review model based on law, institutional policy, medium and potential harm. [N-SPJ][N-OHA]

**Default for commissioned personal stories and commercial testimonials:** obtain informed, appropriately scoped permission to record and publish attributable material; provide accurate context; respect agreed restrictions; assess future identifiable harms; obtain specialized legal/privacy review when exposure is material. This is a **house control**, not a claim that all jurisdictions require the same consent form.

**Default for independent journalism:** protect factual accuracy and minimize harm while preserving editorial independence; permit correction of factual errors but avoid promising editorial veto where it would be inappropriate. Separate journalism ethics from advertising endorsement requirements.

**For children, employees, dependents and vulnerable participants:** apply greater care because consent or participation may not be fully voluntary; weigh power imbalance and foreseeable public exposure. A participant's immediate enthusiasm does not eliminate the publisher's duty to avoid preventable harm.

## 13.3 Fairness and multi-actor stories

Test whether the narrative falsely concentrates credit, assigns an antagonist without evidence, omits relevant stakeholders or makes other parties appear passive props. Fairness does not require identical space for every viewpoint; it requires treatment proportionate to evidentiary relevance and affected interests. When readers could infer a contested claim as settled, attribute or qualify it.

## 13.4 Accuracy versus narrative polish

The following are **publication blockers** for real-life accounts, regardless of aesthetic quality:

- invented dialogue presented as quoted speech;
- nonconsensual sensitive details where publication is not justified/authorized under applicable obligations;
- fabricated or composite individuals presented as particular real individuals;
- invented setbacks, victories, reasons or feelings;
- omission of material counterevidence designed to make an outcome appear established;
- unsupported causal or quantified outcome claims;
- scenario/prediction presented as an already observed event;
- an AI-generated quote attributed to an actual participant without source verification;
- synthetic imagery/audio passed off as authentic contemporaneous evidence where this changes the meaning.

Other issues require context and professional judgment; they are not solved by mechanical checklists alone.

### Trauma-aware interviews: safer questions, not forced exposure

Before the interview, explain subject, intended uses, medium, potential permanence and the option to decline sensitive topics. During it, allow pauses, neutral prompts, boundaries and support where appropriate; do not treat emotional distress as evidence of a story's quality. Afterward, protect exact quotation, contextual accuracy, permissions and correction rights appropriate to the publication regime. Train people doing high-risk interviews; an AI prompt is not a substitute for trauma-informed competence. ## 13.6 V2 indirect-fabrication and selective-emphasis audit

**Literal accuracy is necessary but insufficient.** At review, ask:

1. Has order made a coincidence look like a decisive cause?
2. Has an isolated case implied a representative outcome or customer base rate?
3. Has a single speaker's experience erased competing credible perspectives?
4. Has compression hidden a known qualification, failure, contradiction or confounder?
5. Has scene detail made an uncertain recollection appear contemporaneously recorded?
6. Has a verb or metaphor upgraded a speaker's own interpretation to established fact?
7. Has the ending converted a continuing dispute or early trial into durable success?
8. Could a simple accurate caveat, source attribution or alternative explanation repair the meaning without destroying the story?

**Decision:** `PASS`, `QUALIFY`, `RESTRUCTURE`, `CONTEXTUALIZE`, `HOLD_FOR_SOURCE`, `REMOVE`, or `DECLINE_PUBLICATION`. Do not conceal a necessary correction in small print while leaving a misleading headline, excerpt, short video or social quote intact. [N-NIEMANCLARK04][N-REUTERS][P-WR00]

# 14. Narrative frameworks: useful lenses, not laws

**Evidence boundary:** These are models from classical theory, fiction/screenwriting craft, consulting, branding or presentation practice. Their popularity and recognizability do not establish universal causal effectiveness. Use their own original/fair descriptions for technique, then justify application against real evidence and the reader's objective. Do not mislabel marketing frameworks as peer-reviewed narrative laws.

| Framework | Useful working idea | Situations where it may help | Common category error or failure | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aristotle / classical unified action | A coherent arrangement of action, consequences and a beginning-middle-end relation | Explaining a bounded development; editing a confusing event sequence | Treating classical tragedy as proof all nonfiction needs three acts | `CTX` [N-ARIST] |
| Modern three-act structure | Setup, development and resolution as rough pacing architecture | Longer scripted or tightly plotted stories | Imposing a crisis, midpoint reversal or tidy resolution on real life | `CTX` |
| Joseph Campbell / Hero's Journey | Recurrent transformation motif: departure, ordeal, return (simplified) | Fiction, culturally situated mythic analysis, a genuinely transformative memoir | Claiming all cultures/people/events obey one sequence or casting founder as savior | `CTX` [F-CAMP-CRIT] |
| Dan Harmon / Story Circle | Desire, crossing a boundary, adapting, obtaining, paying, returning changed | Compact scripts, plotted anecdotal experiments | Forcing an unearned cost, reversal or victory | `CTX` [F-HARMON] |
| StoryBrand (Donald Miller) | Customer as hero; brand as guide; problem, plan, stakes, action | Customer-oriented marketing copy when Brand Strategy already defines message | Redefining strategic positioning or inventing customer problems/claims | `CTX` [F-STORYBRAND][P-BRAND][P-WR01] |
| Duarte / Sparkline | Movement between current reality and a possible better future | Some strategic presentations and change talks | Claiming aspiration already realized, or overplaying binary contrast | `CTX` [F-DUARTE] |
| Robert McKee / scene turns | Scenes gain momentum through consequential value changes | Editing longer scene-based nonfiction or drama | Manufacturing reversal or conflict in documented events | `CTX` [F-MCKEE] |
| Labovian personal-experience narrative (descriptive research) | Orientation, complicating action, evaluation, resolution, coda as lenses on *some* natural recounts | Diagnosing why an oral account feels disorienting | Turning descriptive patterns into compulsory writing template | `CTX`, extra source needed for detailed prescription |

## 14.1 Framework selection protocol

1. **Define the actual communication problem.** Is the narrative confusing, too abstract, lacking orientation, missing evidence, too long, or missing emotional context?
2. **Choose the least intervention likely to fix that defect.** A clarified causal connection may solve it without a full plot framework.
3. **Check genre and evidence constraints.** A personal memoir, commercial case, internal vision and short anecdote permit different devices.
4. **Map only events that exist.** If a framework demands a villain, mentor, decisive epiphany or final triumph not present, skip the slot rather than fabricate.
5. **Compare to a simpler structure.** The framework must improve the output under the intended quality criterion.
6. **Reassess against reader evidence.** Strong reader response does not excuse false claims or exploitation.

## 14.2 StoryBrand and Duarte interface rule

WR-02 may organize an already-approved customer or change narrative, but it **does not create brand positioning, approved value propositions, reasons to believe, price promises or CTA authority**. Those inputs remain with Brand Strategy and WR-01. The narrative author may propose a gap or inconsistency for owners to resolve; it MUST NOT silently overwrite the strategy source of truth.

## 14.3 A framework is not an audit result

Passing eight Story Circle beats, three acts, a StoryBrand canvas or a hero's journey checklist proves only structural correspondence. It does **not** demonstrate truth, fairness, comprehension, credibility, memory, customer satisfaction or business effect.

## 14.4 V2 additional frameworks and evidence status

| Model | Actual intellectual/practical job | Evidentiary boundary | Red-team question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Labov & Waletzky** | Descriptive analysis of oral personal-experience stories (abstract, orientation, complication, evaluation, resolution, coda) | Scholarly discourse framework, not a randomized test of best B2B biography structure [N-LABOV] | Does the material naturally contain these functions, or are we manufacturing a complication? |
| **Syd Field / three-act screenplay paradigm** | Beginning, middle and end with plot-point transitions for screenwriting craft | Historical practitioner framework; origin/definition not a universal narrative theorem [N-SYDFIELD] | Does a real event contain an honest turn, or does the template introduce one? |
| **McAdams / redemptive life-story lens** | Describes culturally situated autobiographical narratives of suffering and redemption | Research and cultural interpretation, not a social duty or therapy prescription [N-MCADAMS-BOOK] | Are we coercing a “pain made me stronger” ending? |
| **Harvard Kennedy School / Ganz Public Narrative (Self–Us–Now)** | Leadership and collective commitment framed from self, shared values and a present choice | Useful for specific leadership/organizing jobs; 2026 HKS ethical analyses raise unresolved power, inclusion and manipulation questions [N-GANZ-ETH26] | Who is omitted from “us,” who may refuse “now,” and whose stories were not heard? |

| **Reagan et al.'s six emotional arcs** | Computational classification of lexical sentiment movement in selected Gutenberg fiction | Sample/algorithm-dependent descriptive findings, not six mandatory story plots or a business-performance prediction [N-REAGAN16] | Are we confusing positive/negative word trajectory with real-world causality or worth? |
| **Shell scenarios** | Makes different conditional plausible futures discussable | Operational scenario example, not a universal narrative forecast algorithm [N-SHELL26] | Is a possible path being sold as a guaranteed future? |

### Canonical falsification of the “three-act Aristotle” shortcut

Aristotle's *Poetics* discusses unity and arrangement of tragedy; modern screenwriting three-act page/plot-point templates are a later craft codification. Do not claim Aristotle empirically proved a specific three-act percentage, minute mark or conversion optimum. Syd Field's own school describes its paradigm as adaptable form rather than a page-count prison. [N-ARIST][N-SYDFIELD]

### How to choose, in practice

If the narrative already has a source-supported structure and meets the reader's need, **use no named model**. If it has a specific problem, choose at most the smallest useful lens: Labov for an unclear recollected account; event-indexing for missing time/subject/cause; change narrative for unclear decision and future conditions; case architecture for intervention/results/limits; McKee only for missing progression in an honestly scene-based work. In any discipline, remove the lens when it starts rewriting reality. [N-ZWAAN][P-WR00]

# 15. Application profiles — eight distinct narrative jobs

These profiles are adaptations of the same narrative principles to different reader needs and evidentiary settings, not eight separate specialty playbooks.

## 15.1 Personal narrative

**Reader job:** understand how the speaker experienced an event and what, if anything, changed in their outlook. **Strong material:** an actual choice, encounter, uncertainty, lived detail, changed relationship or unresolved question. **Voice:** the person who lived it; preserve idiosyncrasy rather than marketing polish. **Structure:** reflective or event-led; chronology can be flexible. **Risk:** claiming private certainty or fabricating vulnerability to appear relatable. **Success:** readers understand the real experience without mistaking one interpretation for universal truth.

**Compact example (fictional):** “I expected to enjoy the new role. By the third month I realized I missed building things with my hands. I moved to a smaller team. That decision cost me the title, and gave me work I wanted to keep doing.” This needs no life-changing catastrophe.

## 15.2 Founder story

**Reader job:** understand what the founder noticed, decided, built or learned—and why that history matters to the current company. **Strong material:** observable trigger, choice under constraint, early response, present capabilities and honest unresolved work. **Structure:** problem encountered → specific decision → actual action → evidence → present operating belief. **Risk:** invented “we had nothing but a dream” hardship, inflated origin myths, founder-as-lone-savior, projecting current purpose into every past event. **Success:** a credible connection between authentic history and today's relevant product/service without new unsupported strategic claims.

**Short negative example:** “Against all odds, one visionary dared to disrupt an entire industry.” Unverifiable, empty and generic.

## 15.3 Biography / About

**Reader job:** understand the person's present role, experience and distinctive perspective. **Strong material:** defining choices, work, evidence of capability, selected commitments and relevant personal context. **Default:** **lead with who they are today**, then use the past to explain how they got here. **Risk:** resumé chronology without meaning, excessive private detail or fabricated personal transformation. **Success:** reader knows the person's role and why the experiences are relevant.

**No-story alternative:** for an About panel in a software product, three accurate sentences and a résumé link may be better than a full personal journey.

## 15.4 Company / brand story

**Reader job:** understand what the organization does, why it exists, what it believes *in practice*, and how that translates into relevant value. **Input authority:** category, positioning, promise, proof and distinctive assets from Brand Strategy. **Story role:** make origin, decisions or consequences humanly intelligible. **Risk:** “mission to change the world” clichés, false origin, baseless market claims, overlooking employees/customers. **Success:** a credible narrative that reinforces—not replaces—approved positioning and observable delivery.

A brand story MAY omit origin entirely: a recurring “how we choose to do things” account can be truer and more useful than a forced garage-founder fable.

## 15.5 Customer / case story

**Reader job:** judge what was happening before, what intervention was made, under which constraints and what actually occurred. **Core chain:** context/baseline → customer objective → intervention/implementation → measured and observed results → limitations. **Inputs:** customer approval, records, metrics definitions, dates, attribution, any commercial relationships. **Risks:** selection bias; conflating co-occurrence with causation; extrapolation from one success; fabricated testimonials; strategic exaggeration. **Success:** supports accurate judgment, not just emotional engagement.

**Minimum factual controls:** customer identity or anonymization status; baseline window; outcome window; metric unit and source; implementation scope; concurrent changes; attribution limits; consent/rights; whether outcome is typical or illustrative. For high-stakes effectiveness claims use WR-01's substantiation rules and the measurement specialist.

## 15.6 Strategic / organizational change narrative

**Reader job:** understand what changed, why a decision was made, how it affects them, what remains uncertain and what to do. **Structure:** honest past → evidence about present constraints → choice and trade-offs → near-term steps → uncertainties and feedback. **Risk:** a smooth turnaround story that ignores losses, dissent, personnel consequences or unresolved technical difficulty. **Success:** informed understanding, meaningful consent/feedback where applicable, and realistic expectations.

**Default for employees:** publish explicit practical impacts and decisions first; the historical story is supporting context, not a substitute for pay, role, timing or process facts.

## 15.7 Vision / future narrative

**Reader job:** imagine what could plausibly change, why it matters and what assumptions must hold. **Mode:** aspirational, prospective or scenario—not reporting. **Structure:** verified present starting point → conditional mechanisms → several plausible consequences → what would change the path. **Risk:** inevitability claims, hype, fabricated future user experiences presented as evidence, failure to identify material constraints. **Success:** helps test choices and assumptions rather than merely inspiring belief.

**Future-language controls:** `we aim`, `we plan`, `if X occurs`, `one possible future`, `we forecast using assumptions A/B`, `we commit to deliver Y by Z`. These are not interchangeable.

## 15.8 Short anecdote / micro-narrative

**Reader job:** grasp one narrow point through a concrete event without losing time to a full arc. **Default form:** setup (one sentence) → event/change (one or two) → meaning/qualification (optional one). **Risk:** the anecdote displaces the direct answer; anecdotal result is generalized; punchline becomes false certainty. **Success:** increases orientation or retention at minimal cost.

**Example (fictional):** “The test users understood the feature only after we renamed the button. We changed the label and tested again.” This is enough; inventing a dramatic workshop sequence would add no informational value.

## 15.9 Cross-profile risk matrix

| Profile | Most important verification | Most common AI distortion | Best alternative when story is not needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal | Subject's recollection/attribution and permission | Fake trauma or epiphany | Direct reflection |
| Founder | Documentary origin, credit and current relevance | Hero mythology | Direct company description |
| Biography | Roles, dates, statements and speaker review | Fictional inner life | Short credentialed bio |
| Brand | Approved positioning and proof | Generic world-changing mission | Value proposition + proof |
| Customer case | Baseline, metric, causality, representativeness | Invented quantified outcome | Evidence table |
| Strategy/change | Decision, affected groups, alternatives, uncertainty | Inevitability/consensus narrative | Decision memo + FAQ |

## 15.10 V2 application contracts: exact inputs, truth risks and desired output

| Profile | Essential evidence to collect | Narrative question | Preferred output | Hard failure to prevent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Personal narrative** | Speaker's own memories, dates/anchor points where relevant, named boundaries, affected people | “What did I experience and what changed in my interpretation?” | First-person bounded recollection, including uncertainty or unresolved meaning | Invented motive for others; coerced confession; fake redemption |
| **Founder story** | Verified chronology, decisions, co-founder/team contributions, original constraints, actual trade-offs | “Which real decisions explain the venture's present focus?” | Selected origin/decision episodes plus present-day clarity | Myth of solitary genius, near-bankruptcy invention, hindsight-as-foresight |
| **Biography/About** | Role, credible experience, current practice, selected illustrative moments | “What must the reader know about this person to understand them?” | Often direct factual bio plus one telling episode, or no story | Endless childhood arc delaying role clarity |
| **Company/brand story** | Brand Strategy authority, history, actual product/service proof, stakeholder roles | “How did the organization's way of solving the problem develop?” | Short factual description plus verified origin/change if useful | Unauthorized positioning or aspirational claims written as achievements |
| **Customer case** | Baseline, intervention, timeline, outcomes, counterfactors, customer approval, constraints | “What happened in this bounded customer's context?” | Challenge → actual work → evidenced result → limitations; not formulaic triumph | Fabricated numbers, guarantee implied from one case, selected winners hidden |
| **Strategic/change narrative** | Decision/mandate, real constraints, impacted groups, hard choices, benefits/costs, next steps | “Why this change, for whom, with what uncertainty and agency?” | Direct decision first, causal background, credible options, action and open questions | Inspirational gloss over job losses, disagreement, risks or timeline |
| **Vision/future narrative** | Current baseline, underlying assumptions, uncertainties, alternative scenarios, ambition/ownership | “What could become possible, under which choices and risks?” | Vision or scenario explicitly signposted; evidence for premises | Fictional future presented as observed, inevitable, or already funded |
| **Short anecdote** | One observable event, speaker attribution, why this fact helps now | “Can this particular moment clarify one point quickly?” | One compact passage, followed by direct relevance if helpful | Anecdote outrunning the reader question or being treated as representative data |

**Past–present–future example, hypothetical company case:**

> *Past:* “In 2024 the support team answered requests manually. Internal logs show recurring handoffs and delays.” *Present:* “In 2026 a central queue now routes new cases and the team measures time to resolution weekly.” *Future:* “If adoption grows as expected, management plans to trial specialist routing; that pilot has not been approved or shown to improve outcomes yet.”

The narrative ties a continuing question (how cases are handled) through distinct time statuses. It does not invent a villain, decisive overnight transformation or guaranteed future. Its exact fictional dates/measurements are **teaching examples only**, not real-world claims.

**Short About anti-example → correction (synthetic).**

- Weak: “Since childhood, I knew I was destined to change the world. Then everything fell apart, and I discovered my superpower.” Unsupported theatrical arc; unclear present role.
- Better: “I work on new business concepts at the intersection of technology and operations. After building the first version of a customer-support workflow, I saw how much depended on clearer handoffs between people and tools.” A narrower source-compatible observation; if even that anecdote cannot be verified, state the role directly and remove it.

**Strategic change anti-example → correction (synthetic).**

- Weak: “The old way held us back. Everyone embraced our transformation, and today we are unstoppable.” No specific policy, evidence, employee experience or uncertainty.
- Better: “We are combining two support teams. The goal is more consistent response coverage; we expect a transition period with training needs, and we will review wait times and staff workload after eight weeks.” Keeps a decision, conditional rationale, impact and testable follow-up. If no such decision exists, do not invent one. [N-AOM16][N-HBR23]

# 16. AI-assisted narrative production: protections and failure modes

## 16.1 The AI's proper role

Generative AI MAY help with event ordering, outlining, alternative structures, selective shortening, gap detection, clarifying transitions, questions for interviews, counterfactual objections and copyediting. It MUST NOT be treated as witness, primary source, authority for a person's motives, or independent verifier of its own generated details. Research on AI-assisted creativity finds task-dependent improvement in judged individual outputs accompanied by possible diversity/homogenization costs across outputs; neither effect justifies an absolute ban or blind adoption. [N-AICREATE][N-AIHOMO]

## 16.2 AI failure taxonomy

| Failure | Observable symptom | Control or correct response |
|---|---|---|
| `INVENTED_DRAMA` | “Everything was on the line” without record | Remove; restore actual stakes |
| `FORCED_CONFLICT` | Characters assigned adversarial positions they did not hold | Replace with actual constraint or absence of conflict |
| `FAKE_VULNERABILITY` | Invented grief, fear, doubt or insecurity | Attribute lived feeling only when expressed by source |
| `GENERIC_INSPIRATION` | “A journey of courage, resilience and passion” | Return to concrete event, choice and consequence |
| `FABRICATED_SCENE` | Imagined weather, room, dialogue, gestures | Require source for each detail; otherwise summarize |
| `OMNISCIENT_MINDREADING` | Claims what real subjects privately knew/wanted | Use sourced testimony or observation |
| `FORMULA_LOCK_IN` | Every story has 3 acts, villain, epiphany and win | Choose structure from actual material; allow no-story |
| `CLICHÉ_METAPHOR` | “Roller coaster,” “tapestry,” “journey” without diagnostic meaning | Remove or use original sourced image |
| `CAUSAL_LAUNDERING` | Timeline reframed as intervention-caused result | Mark alternative explanations; downgrade wording |
| `PROOF_LAUNDERING` | Invented customers, quotes, metrics or trends | Block publication until independently corroborated |
| `FUTURE_AS_FACT` | Vision scene read as guarantee | Label hypothetical/assumptions; separate commitments |
| `VOICE_FLATTENING` | Human speech rewritten as templated leadership prose | Source-to-output voice diff; preserve justified irregularity |
| `OVEREXPLAINED_MORAL` | Every episode ends in a universal lesson | Let the event carry meaning; use restraint |
| `HERO_ERASURE` | Founder credited for collective work | Restore relevant collaborators and conditions |
| `CONTENT_FLOOD` | Every slide, email or section becomes a story | Re-run Story-or-Not Decision and cut low-value arcs |
| `MISLEADING_OMISSION` | AI trims caveats to “improve flow” | Material-facts ledger and implied-meaning review |

## 16.3 Conservative execution contract for AI

```text
ROLE: Narrative editor and structure analyst. Not a source of facts.
INPUT: Authorized evidence ledger, source excerpts, speaker voice sample,
       audience/job, declared truth mode, context and constraints.
REQUIRED FIRST OUTPUT:
  1. State whether a narrative is useful; allow NO_STORY.
  2. Identify the observed event chain and unresolved gaps.
  3. Name any facts/causal claims absent from sources.
  4. Recommend the smallest appropriate narrative intervention.
WRITING RULES:
  - Use only supported facts; never invent quotes, dialogue, sensation,
    motive, emotions, dates, numbers, customers or outcomes.
  - Separate observations, attributed testimony, inference and scenario.
  - Preserve distinctive speaker voice and successful existing passages.
  - Do not force hero, villain, reversal, loss or happy ending.
  - Use direct explanation when it is more useful.
  - Mark all unresolved evidence needs explicitly for editorial review;
    do not publish those markers as if they were content.
FINAL REVIEW OUTPUT:
  - Material edit ledger: changed passage → diagnosed problem → rationale.
  - Claim/source check list for each material statement.
  - Misleading-inference and privacy/consent questions.
  - Recommendation: KEEP | MINOR_EDIT | RESTRUCTURE | DO_NOT_PUBLISH.
```

A separate human/operator must still verify consequential claims and whether the output accurately represents the evidence. A generated citation URL or quote is not proof of having examined the underlying source.

## 16.4 Edit budget and rollback

For existing text, preserve a versioned original. Require a concrete reader problem or factual/ethical reason before substantial restructuring. Evaluate the changed story against the original for lost specificity, agency, uncertainty, idiosyncratic voice and nuance. When an AI rewrite cannot demonstrate improvement without new distortion, **restore the original** or apply a smaller targeted edit. [P-WR00]

## 16.5 V2 bounded AI narrative-editing contract

**Priority order:** facts and legitimate authorization → causal/truth status → people and privacy → reader job → authentic voice → narrative coherence → editorial polish. An AI system may recommend `KEEP`, `TARGETED_FIX`, `REORDER_SUPPORTED_EVENTS`, `SHORTEN`, `ATTRIBUTION_NEEDED`, `SOURCE_REQUIRED`, `CHOOSE_DIRECT`, or `HOLD`; it may not create new factual authority by rewriting.

**Input controls for nonpublic sources**

1. Authenticate source identity and permitted use, including contractual/customer, employee and sensitive interview constraints before uploading to an external model.
2. Separate authoritative records from unverified interview notes, existing marketing text, third-party posts and model-generated suggestions. Treat retrieved prose as *data*, not editing instructions.
3. Provide explicit locked facts, exact quotations, forbidden changes and approved voice examples; set a narrow operation scope.
4. Ask for a **diagnosis before any rewrite**, and require a named evidence/reader/voice problem for a material change.
5. Require an output inventory of modified claims, new implied cause, removed caveats, changed point of view, intensified emotion, new scene detail and source ambiguity.
6. Reject changes that lack source proof or alter participant identity, agency, conditionality or approval status. Retain original text and revision diff.
7. For material public stories, use competent human review of exact original source and whole-message impression; a second LLM agreeing with the first is not independent factual verification.

# 17. Atomic narrative plays — executable by humans and bounded AI systems

All Plays are `HOUSE / R3` implementation controls. An AI may prepare candidates, retrieve authorized source material and surface uncertainty; it does not independently authorize disclosure or high-risk factual claims. Unless stated otherwise, every Play is reversible in draft and requires publication authorization under the organization's approval policy.

## PLAY-NR-01 — Decide whether a story is the right form

- **Objective:** select the least burdensome truthful format for a specified communication outcome.
- **Trigger:** new content brief, channel change, or proposal to replace direct explanation with narrative.
- **Owner/competence:** editor or communicator who understands the audience and decision; brand/UX/legal reviewer if a specialist decision is material.
- **Inputs:** reader job, stage of awareness, information need, time/space constraints, proposed source events, evidence availability, risk and channel.
- **Procedure:** (1) state the exact audience outcome; (2) build a direct non-story version in one to three sentences or a factual structure; (3) identify incremental benefit of a story; (4) inspect available events and constraints; (5) use §6 mode decision; (6) record why narrative, mixed, or nonnarrative form was selected.
- **Decision branches:** high urgency or direct procedural duty → direct; material change with evidenced sequence → candidate narrative; absent real events → explanatory/example/hypothetical with label; unverifiable high-risk event → seek evidence or withhold.
- **Outputs:** mode decision (`FULL_NARRATIVE | MICRO_NARRATIVE | EXPLANATION_WITH_ANECDOTE | DIRECT_EXPLANATION | EVIDENCE_SUMMARY | PROCEDURE | DO_NOT_PUBLISH`) and one-sentence rationale.
- **Acceptance/verification:** a reviewer can name what the chosen mode adds; it does not delay or obscure necessary facts; truth-status is correct.
- **Stop/escalate:** narrative appears to require invented turning point, confidential details, or an inappropriate emotional hook.
- **Learning trigger:** reader task failure, audience misinterpretation or performance evidence favoring the alternative format.

## PLAY-NR-02 — Discover a truthful story

- **Objective:** assemble enough source material to support the intended nonfiction narrative without filling gaps imaginatively.
- **Trigger:** approved narrative job based on actual people/events.
- **Owner/competence:** experienced interviewer/researcher for published nonfiction; domain, legal/privacy or factual specialist for consequential subjects.
- **Inputs:** event records, interviews, approvals, notes, existing copy, relevant external evidence and narrative context.
- **Procedure:** (1) inventory sources and permissions; (2) establish original situation and time range; (3) extract event claims separately from interpretations; (4) interview using open, non-leading prompts; (5) identify conflicting accounts, unavailable witnesses and alternative explanations; (6) mark fact classes from §7; (7) document any missing facts that could reverse meaning.
- **Decision branches:** material claim unsupported → seek source/downgrade/remove; participant denies attribution → respect applicable obligations/escalate; evidence conflicts → attribute difference or investigate; source unsafe to expose → redact, anonymize or decline publication.
- **Outputs:** evidence ledger, permissions/risk notes, usable event inventory, unresolved question list.
- **Acceptance/verification:** every high-salience event or quote has an evidence reference and truth-status; public use is allowed within the specified scope.
- **Stop/escalate:** unreliable fabricated evidence, privacy risk beyond editorial authority, contested allegation or impossible verification of a decisive claim.
- **Learning:** corrections and source disputes update the canonical ledger and published version.

## PLAY-NR-03 — Build the causal and temporal spine

- **Objective:** establish a coherent account of what changed without mistaking sequence for cause.
- **Trigger:** story discovery complete enough to select events.
- **Owner/competence:** narrator/editor plus relevant domain reviewer for technical, scientific or economic outcomes.
- **Inputs:** event inventory with dates/confidence, goals, observed consequences and counterevidence.
- **Procedure:** (1) create a factual timeline; (2) mark each claimed causal connection as documented, attributed, inferred or unknown; (3) list alternative mechanisms; (4) identify the main throughline; (5) select only events that change or illuminate that throughline; (6) choose chronological or justified nonlinear presentation; (7) draft a one-paragraph synopsis using appropriately qualified causal language.
- **Decision branches:** causality unverified → chronological or attributed narration; time sequence ambiguous → add date/anchor; unresolved present → open ending; future extension → label intention, projection or scenario.
- **Outputs:** source-linked timeline, causal map and throughline statement.
- **Acceptance/verification:** a reviewer can reconstruct the event order and distinguish known from inferred causes; omitted events do not materially reverse the meaning.
- **Stop/escalate:** a central causal premise collapses when competing explanations are considered.
- **Learning:** later evidence triggers a causal-wording review, not merely a cosmetic update.

## PLAY-NR-04 — Design the narrative structure

- **Objective:** choose progression, focalization, scene/summary distribution and ending that fit the actual material.
- **Trigger:** event spine is evidence-supported and narrative mode confirmed.
- **Owner/competence:** writer/editor; subject reviewer where first-person testimony is attributed.
- **Inputs:** audience outcome, spine, source detail, declared truth mode, voice samples, length and medium.
- **Procedure:** (1) choose opening orientation; (2) locate actual tension, constraint or question, if any; (3) select scenes only where documented detail supports them; (4) use exposition for summary and context; (5) order revelations without hiding necessary truth; (6) choose honest outcome or open question; (7) compare against a shorter direct version; (8) optionally test one framework from §14 only for a known structural defect.
- **Decision branches:** scene needs invented evidence → replace with summary; theme feels imposed → remove editorial moral; narrative drifts from approved strategy → return to source owner; no reader gain → revert to direct form.
- **Outputs:** compact outline with evidence references and truth-status at each beat.
- **Acceptance/verification:** event progression intelligible, no fabricated obligatory beat, all major sections advance the throughline.
- **Stop/escalate:** structure gives a false impression of certainty, efficacy, emotional state or common experience.
- **Learning:** assess actual reader comprehension and misconstrual, not just whether beats are present.

## PLAY-NR-05 — Draft and selectively revise

- **Objective:** produce clear, distinctive, appropriately paced narrative without losing source accuracy or the author's voice.
- **Trigger:** sufficient source-backed outline or an existing narrative with diagnosed flaws.
- **Owner/competence:** writer or bounded AI assistant supervised by editor; speaker review for first-person ghostwriting as appropriate.
- **Inputs:** source ledger, outline, voice source, current draft, constraints and identified reader problem.
- **Procedure:** (1) preserve working original text; (2) draft only supported content; (3) use concrete sourced detail selectively; (4) read for causal, thematic and temporal continuity; (5) identify each material change to existing copy and its reason; (6) eliminate cliches, formulaic hooks, unsupported morals and duplicate messages; (7) perform a semantic diff to detect loss of uncertainty and agency; (8) restore any original passage the rewrite did not demonstrably improve.
- **Decision branches:** distinctive but imperfect voice works → preserve; reader cannot orient → targeted restructuring; AI invented context → remove and re-verify; no clear defect → no change.
- **Outputs:** versioned candidate and change rationale.
- **Acceptance/verification:** consistent voice, transparent causal claims, no factual drift or gratuitous complication; WR-00 writing gates pass.
- **Stop/escalate:** speaker identity misrepresented or material meaning changes without authorization.
- **Learning:** track categories of edits later reverted and update AI editing instructions.

## PLAY-NR-06 — Verify, protect and authorize real-life publication

- **Objective:** ensure the entire story and its likely implications are fair, sourced and ethically publishable.
- **Trigger:** public, customer-facing, employee-facing or high-consequence draft approaches publication.
- **Owner/competence:** competent editor/fact checker; domain/legal/privacy specialist when triggered; appropriate publisher authorization.
- **Inputs:** candidate, factual ledger, source material, rights and participant context, approved claims/brand inputs, intended distribution.
- **Procedure:** (1) fact-check high-consequence nouns, numbers, time, quotation and imagery; (2) challenge every causal and motive claim; (3) verify disclosure/consent where applicable; (4) run counter-story and omitted-facts test; (5) evaluate case selection and representativeness; (6) assess foreseeable harms; (7) run realistic implied-meaning review; (8) correct or reject; (9) record reviewer and release decision.
- **Decision branches:** unsupported material truth claim → `BLOCK`; nonmaterial ambiguity → correct/qualify; unresolved legal/privacy exposure → `HOLD`; aesthetic preference alone → do not compel rewrite; truth and proportionality pass → `APPROVE` by authorized owner.
- **Outputs:** evidence-linked publication decision and unresolved-risk register.
- **Acceptance/verification:** no known material misinformation or hidden status error; legal/ethical overlays addressed; authorization recorded.
- **Stop/escalate:** fabricated proof, source tampering, inappropriate sensitive disclosure, deceptive case framing or unverified severe allegation.
- **Learning:** postpublication corrections feed source register, revisions and editorial training.

## PLAY-NR-07 — Create a strategic or future narrative responsibly

- **Objective:** link known past and present to conditional plausible futures without false inevitability.
- **Trigger:** organizational change, founder vision, future roadmap, change-management presentation or scenario writing.
- **Owner/competence:** accountable strategy owner for assumptions; editor for representation; specialists for material regulated/financial/technical predictions.
- **Inputs:** verified past, current capability/constraints, stated strategy, decision assumptions, scenarios, affected parties and uncertainty.
- **Procedure:** (1) distinguish evidence from strategic choice; (2) articulate a single throughline across time; (3) state current facts before projected outcomes; (4) identify contingencies, external dependencies and trade-offs; (5) label future sentences as aspiration/plan/commitment/forecast/scenario; (6) include at least one material alternative or failure condition where omission would distort; (7) test with people affected by the proposed change.
- **Decision branches:** deterministic promised result unsupported → condition/downgrade; reader needs precise decision and timeline → lead with memo/direct statement; material staff impact unresolved → do not use aspirational copy as substitute for disclosure.
- **Outputs:** clearly labeled narrative plus assumptions and practical decisions.
- **Acceptance/verification:** future does not masquerade as past fact; the affected audience understands choices, risks and next steps.
- **Stop/escalate:** false commitment, concealment of significant adverse impact or unreviewed predictive claim.
- **Learning:** compare subsequent events to assumptions and publicly correct outdated factual premises when relevant.

## PLAY-NR-08 — Evaluate narrative versus alternatives

- **Objective:** learn whether narrative improves intended outcomes without confusing aesthetic approval with success.
- **Trigger:** material narrative investment, channel-level change, high-profile story, disputed format or recurring underperformance.
- **Owner/competence:** editor + UX/research or measurement specialist appropriate to sampling and causal inference.
- **Inputs:** defined user job, outcome metrics, baseline/non-story alternative, source-compliant variants, population, harm guardrails.
- **Procedure:** (1) state the decision and target construct; (2) compare a narrative to a *fair, information-equivalent* direct alternative; (3) predefine important outcomes, smallest meaningful difference and nonnegotiable factuality; (4) choose suitable comprehension/usability study, audience interview, controlled experiment or operational monitoring; (5) sample relevant audiences; (6) measure errors and false inferences as well as recall/engagement; (7) interpret uncertainty and alternative explanations; (8) keep, adapt or stop based on net reader benefit and cost.
- **Decision branches:** low stakes, low cost → targeted qualitative test; causal performance claim → appropriately powered randomized test if feasible; insufficient sample → inconclusive rather than “proven”; factual/ethical fail → reject regardless of engagement.
- **Outputs:** evaluation record with decision, limitations, ownership and review date.
- **Acceptance/verification:** method measures intended outcome; format variants are meaningfully comparable; claims stay inside experimental scope.
- **Stop/escalate:** adverse trust/misunderstanding effect, privacy issue, instrumentation failure, contamination or misleading selective reporting.
- **Learning:** update the specific contextual recommendation, not a universal storytelling rule from one local test.

## PLAY-NR-09 — Challenge the story's implicit proposition and counter-narratives

- **Objective:** identify where a literally accurate narrative could create a materially unsupported overall impression.
- **Use when:** public customer/founder/organizational narrative, disputed context, or a case used to justify material decisions.
- **Do not use when:** no material implication risk beyond ordinary WR-00 checks; escalate direct allegations to qualified professionals instead of untrained solo review.
- **Preconditions/roles:** versioned draft; editor who has not authored the critical causal claim if practical; access to event ledger and contrary records; decision owner identified.
- **Inputs:** claim/edge map, original sources, material omissions, audience/job, competing perspectives and actual approval status.
- **Method:** (1) ask what a reasonable uninformed reader will conclude; (2) separately annotate explicit and implied claims; (3) reverse key event order/selection hypothetically to find hidden emphasis effects; (4) check plausible alternative causal explanations and who benefits from the framing; (5) compare with a source-faithful neutral summary; (6) name necessary corrections; (7) repeat reader-inference probes after revisions.
- **Decision logic:** unsupported implication → add source/status/context or restructure; missing consent/rights for material disclosure → HOLD; a newly sourced alternative changes the headline meaning → rewrite or decline; cosmetic objection alone → KEEP.
- **Guardrails:** do not represent contested viewpoints as equally evidenced; do not reveal confidential counter-sources to unauthorized parties; do not pressure vulnerable sources for dramatic details.
- **Output:** explicit/implicit proposition ledger and `PASS | REVISE | HOLD | REJECT` disposition with owner.
- **Acceptance:** reviewer can justify headline, chronology, causal verbs, focalization and omitted material in terms of supported evidence and reader effect; no material false inference left unaddressed.
- **Failure/recovery:** missing source or irreconcilable contested implication → stop release, seek qualified review, preserve original and document open risk.
- **Evidence/learning:** source links + annotated diff + reviewer disposition; reader misunderstanding or later corrections trigger regression review. [N-NIEMANCLARK04][N-AOM16][P-MPS]

## PLAY-NR-10 — Produce an accurate real-person or customer narrative

- **Objective:** make one real bounded lived sequence intelligible without overstating outcome or compromising people.
- **Use when:** customer case, founder/employee biography, personal testimony or oral-history-derived content.
- **Do not use when:** central events cannot be supported or material use rights are missing.
- **Preconditions/roles:** subject/source identity, purpose, stakes, publisher and approval route identified; a privacy/rights specialist available when consequence warrants.
- **Inputs:** authorized interview/records, chronology, relevant quotes, approved brand claims if commercial, project-specific consent/rights, source-status labels.
- **Method:** (1) choose the reader's legitimate question; (2) check direct form; (3) map event and claim ledger; (4) characterize actual situation and choice, including collective agency; (5) narrate only source-supported progression; (6) qualify measurable outcomes and uncertainty; (7) retain source and publisher distinction for first-person testimony; (8) check identity and implied typicality; (9) authorize; (10) preserve future correction contact.
- **Decision logic:** private or potentially harmful disclosure unnecessary → remove; outcome unmeasured → avoid quantitative/causal wording; dialogue unverified → paraphrase/omit; speaker refuses an agreed use → follow contractual/legal and ethics process; no narrative advantage → direct biography/case fact sheet.
- **Guardrails:** never create trauma, a heroic ending or a synthetic quote; no undisclosed character composite in factual journalism; don't give a sponsor unauthorized veto over investigative coverage.
- **Output:** source-backed narrative, rights status, causal/typicality disclosures and publish/hold decision.
- **Acceptance:** subject rights honored according to context; every decisive event/quote has lineage; first-person perspective is authorized; reader can identify true outcome and limits.
- **Failure/recovery:** stop/withdraw/correct any publication that misidentifies a subject, violates a binding restriction, or implies unsupported results; notify responsible owners as necessary.
- **Evidence/learning:** claim ledger, source dates, permissions and correction trail; record reasons for participant objections without assuming objections always grant publication veto. [N-OHA][N-REUTERS][P-WR01]

## PLAY-NR-11 — Compare narrative with a fair non-narrative alternative

- **Objective:** test whether narrative format improves the specific outcome enough to justify its added effort and risk.
- **Use when:** consequential scaled public communication, strategic/organization-wide dissemination, or uncertainty about storytelling necessity.
- **Do not use when:** risky/deceptive unverified content; a controlled experiment would expose people to material harm; ordinary small copy revisions lacking worthwhile evaluation value.
- **Preconditions/roles:** both variants meet truth/privacy gates; research/measurement competence and decision rights; no known unequal treatment of material participants.
- **Inputs:** intended users, predeclared primary outcome, two fact-equivalent versions, exposure and measurement method, comparator rationale.
- **Method:** (1) predefine the task and smallest meaningful advantage; (2) align central facts/proof/claim/CTA across variants; (3) document differences in length, emotional content, visual presentation and production quality; (4) randomize suitable populations where appropriate, or conduct structured qualitative comprehension tests without causal claims; (5) measure correctness, false inference, action and harm; (6) analyze by allocation for randomized designs, account for missingness/delay; (7) choose evidence-limited decision and document uncertainty.
- **Decision logic:** narrative beats direct on engagement but harms comprehension → FAIL/REVISE; no material difference → prefer simpler lower-maintenance form or retain brand-fit choice; direction uncertain → INCONCLUSIVE and stop overclaiming; severe misleading impression → cease exposure, correct.
- **Guardrails:** avoid treatment/no-message asymmetry unless no-message is itself the policy comparator; protect vulnerable or high-stakes groups; no universal “winning story” uplift expectation.
- **Output:** evaluation record, decision, residual limits and deployment/learning plan.
- **Acceptance:** actual outcome assessed with relevant denominator and guardrails; comparison does not systematically favor story by giving it more information; conclusions within tested population/medium.
- **Failure/recovery:** instrumentation failure or contamination → declare invalid/inconclusive, do not claim uplift; risk to people → halt and seek authority.
- **Evidence/learning:** preserve variants, allocation protocol, measures and analysis; rerun when audience/source/format materially changes. [N-RAH25][P-MEAS][P-MPS]

# 18. Worked examples and anti-examples

**Important:** All illustrative people, companies, customers, metrics and scenes in this section are **fictional teaching examples**. They are not testimonials, external evidence, verified cases or performance promises. The goal is to show editorial reasoning and the kind of correction needed.

## Example A — A sequence of facts versus an explanatory story

**Anti-example (timeline only):** “In February the team ran interviews. In March it changed onboarding. In April adoption rose.”

**Problem:** this is a perfectly legitimate timeline, but it does not explain what the interviews revealed, why the team changed its approach, or whether adoption growth was caused by the change. Calling it a “story of transformation” would be unjustified.

**Better when the record supports the mechanism:** “During interviews, customers said the setup screen used terms they did not recognize. In March, the team replaced those labels with language customers used. Adoption rose in April, although the team had not isolated the change from other factors.”

**What changed:** one sourced potential mechanism was added, and the causal result was restrained. If the interview record were missing, this rewrite would be *worse*, not better.

## Example B — Founder origin without heroic fiction

**Anti-example:** “Armed with nothing but an impossible dream, the founder risked everything to reinvent the industry.”

**Better:** “While working with small manufacturers, the founder repeatedly saw orders managed through email attachments. She built a shared order view with two pilot customers. The product has since changed, but that original problem still guides which features the team builds.”

**Why:** specific origin → actual action → current throughline. The improvement presupposes all facts were verified; it does not justify inserting them if the source is silent.

## Example C — About text when no story is necessary

**Anti-example:** “My journey has always been about the limitless pursuit of excellence, from humble beginnings to transformative leadership.”

**Better DIRECT form:** “I design operations for early-stage teams. My recent work has focused on reducing handoffs and documenting recurring decisions. I advise founders on turning those practices into repeatable systems.”

**Decision:** no story. The reader needs current role and relevance; manufactured personal history is a distraction.

## Example D — Respect a small personal shift

**Anti-example:** “The rejection shattered her. In that darkest moment, she discovered her true destiny.”

**Better:** “The role went to another applicant. She felt disappointed, took several weeks to reconsider what kind of work suited her, and applied to a smaller team.”

**Why:** proportionate and non-heroic; only use emotional attribution if genuinely sourced to the subject.

## Example E — Customer case without effect laundering

**Anti-example:** “Our platform delivered 60% faster growth in three weeks. Here's how it revolutionized the business.”

**Better with limited evidence:** “The customer introduced the platform during a quarter when it also changed its sales process. Lead-handling time fell in the same period. Because several changes occurred together, the team cannot attribute the full improvement to the platform.”

**Why:** a case can still be informative when it does not prove causation. Exact percentages are omitted here because none was supplied as evidence. Claims, consent and metric definitions require specialist review.

## Example F — Strategic change: concrete information before narrative

**Anti-example:** “Every great transformation begins with the courage to embrace a new chapter. Together, we will emerge stronger.”

**Better:** “From January, support requests will be handled by one shared team rather than four regional queues. The change aims to reduce duplicate handoffs, but may lengthen response times during the transition. Staff roles and escalation contacts are listed below. Last year's review of repeated handoffs explains why we are making this change.”

**Why:** affected readers can act; context explains the decision without hiding consequences or promising success.

## Example G — Future scenario versus certainty

**Anti-example:** “By 2030 every customer will have an AI assistant, and work will finally be effortless.”

**Better SCENARIO:** “One possible 2030 operating model gives employees access to an assistant that handles routine administrative tasks. That outcome depends on reliable integration, governance, user acceptance and cost. The scenario is an exploration, not a forecast.”

**Why:** possible future, actual dependencies and no invented certainty. Use concrete, sourced constraints for consequential planning.

## Example H — Scene reconstruction and truth

**Anti-example:** “At 7:42 a.m., cold rain hit the windows as he whispered, ‘This is our last chance.’”

**Problem:** timestamp, weather, gesture and quote were generated for cinematic impact, not documented.

**Better:** “The team met that morning to decide whether to continue the project. Meeting notes record concerns about the remaining budget. They agreed to run one further customer test before committing more funds.”

**Why:** a meaningful decision survives without fictional sensory detail.

## Example I — A representative fact after a powerful anecdote

**Anti-example:** “Maya's one-week recovery proves the treatment works for almost everyone.”

**Better when appropriate records exist:** “Maya reported improvement after one week. Her experience illustrates a possible response, but does not establish how often patients improve, what caused her improvement or what others should expect.”

**Why:** testimony and general medical effectiveness are separate evidentiary questions. A clinical communication would require specialist medical source review.

## Example J — Micro-anecdote that does not hijack the answer

**Question:** “Why do we include a test user in release reviews?”

**Poor response:** five paragraphs of dramatic origin before giving an answer.

**Better:** “A representative user can expose assumptions the team doesn't notice. For example, in a hypothetical release, the checklist passed but a new user could not find the main action. That kind of test checks real comprehension rather than only technical completion.”

**Why:** answer first; illustrative anecdote second; hypothetical clearly labeled.

## Example K — A quiet story with no obligatory lesson

**Anti-example ending:** “And that day taught us the ultimate truth that real innovation is never about technology but about humanity.”

**Better ending:** “The team kept the old option for another month. Some customers still preferred it.”

**Why:** the real unresolved tension is more credible than an unsupported universal moral.

## Example L — Third-person narrator must not read minds

**Anti-example:** “She smiled, secretly knowing the merger would fail.”

**Better:** “She later said she had been concerned about the merger's timeline. At the meeting, she asked for a contingency plan.”

**Why:** attributed private report + observed behavior replace invented omniscience.

## Example M — Past, present and future as one question

**Disconnected version:** “We started in 2020. Now we have new software. Our vision is to change the world.”

**Coherent, conditional version:** “In our early projects, a single specialist often held all the handover knowledge. We now record those decisions in a shared system. Our next experiment is whether the same approach helps new colleagues work independently; we have not tested that outcome at scale.”

**Throughline:** where knowledge lives and whether it can transfer. The future is a testable aspiration, not a guaranteed destiny.

## Example N — Multi-actor credit

**Anti-example:** “One visionary leader saved the service.”

**Better:** “Staff developed a workaround during the outage. The operations team later formalized it, and management approved the required resources.”

**Why:** direct actions and responsibilities replace individual mythology.

## Example O — Stop when narrative costs more than it contributes

**Request:** “Write an evocative story to tell users how to reset a password.”

**Decision:** reject full narrative. Publish the reset steps with a short explanation about verification and recovery. A decorative story increases navigation cost and may obscure urgent security instructions.

## Example P — Narrative can work without conflict

**Anti-example:** “The team fought its way through impossible odds to reach agreement.”

**Better:** “The team met every Friday for six weeks. Each meeting resolved one disagreement about terminology. By the final session, the shared glossary had become the reference for new projects.”

**Why:** steady accumulation itself can constitute meaningful progression; no invented confrontation is needed.

## V2 supplementary examples Q–X (synthetic; educational, not factual case claims)

**Q — A memorable opening that delays vital information.** **Anti-example:** “Before I tell you what changed in our leave policy, imagine a storm approaching the harbor...” in an HR email. **Better:** “From 1 November, unused vacation days follow the updated rollover rule. The changes and exceptions are below.” A brief explanation of why the policy changed may follow. **Reason:** high-consequence action/instruction wins over a narrative hook. [P-WR00]

**R — False causality introduced by editing.** **Anti-example:** “We launched a mentoring scheme; six months later retention rose, proving mentoring works.” **Better:** “Retention rose after the mentoring scheme began. We also changed pay bands, so the contribution of mentoring is not yet established.” **Reason:** preserve alternate causes and bound the inference. [P-MEAS]

**S — Future vision told as an achievement.** **Anti-example:** “By 2029 everyone will receive instant help from our AI team.” **Better:** “Our goal is faster support. One possible 2029 scenario is around-the-clock first response, conditional on product reliability, user consent and operational capacity.” **Reason:** ambition does not establish a forecast. [N-SHELL26]

**T — Customer case with selection distortion.** **Anti-example:** “Meet our client who cut processing time in half—what our customers can expect.” **Better:** “In this customer's selected pilot, measured processing time decreased by half over the reported window. The pilot was small, and outcomes may differ elsewhere.” **Reason:** a sourced result can still imply unsupported typicality. Include baseline, denominator and confounds if commercially material. [P-WR01]

**U — Biographical story without a crisis.** **Anti-example:** “She had to overcome an impossible obstacle to discover her mission.” **Better:** “Her interest emerged over several projects: first observing the problem, then testing approaches with colleagues, then changing how she works.” **Reason:** accumulation is a legitimate kind of change; do not demand adversity. [N-MCADAMS-BOOK]

**V — Multi-person organizational narrative.** **Anti-example:** “The CEO invented a new support process, and the team followed.” **Better:** “Customer-support staff surfaced recurring handoffs, operations mapped bottlenecks and leadership funded a pilot. Employees disagreed about the rollout speed, so the trial includes feedback checkpoints.” **Reason:** agency and dissent are not narrative noise. [N-AOM16]

**W — Compelled vulnerability from a participant.** **Anti-example:** “To make the video compelling, please describe the worst moment of your illness.” **Better:** “You're free to describe only the parts of your experience you want included; we can focus on what would help other people understand the service and its limits.” **Reason:** a real-person story is not license to extract personal trauma. [N-OHA][N-DART-INTERVIEW]

**X — Narrative versus information-equivalent explanation.** **Version A, narrative:** “A small team tracked requests in several inboxes. When cases started getting lost between groups, they piloted a shared queue. After launch, the team monitored response times.” **Version B, direct:** “The team replaced separate request inboxes with a shared queue to reduce handoff errors. It monitored response times after deployment.” **Decision:** both convey the same main facts; compare readers' understanding and time cost before declaring one superior. Neither version says the unmeasured improvement occurred. [N-RAH25][N-MAR21]

# 19. Evaluation, quality gates and publication acceptance

## 19.1 Quality dimensions — not one weighted score

Each material narrative should be reviewed for the distinct constructs below. An aesthetically powerful story can fail truthfulness; a factually accurate story can mislead through omission; a beautifully structured narrative can be inferior to a direct explanation. Do not combine these into a universal “story quality = 92/100.”

| Dimension | Diagnostic | Example of observable evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Reader/task fit | Did a story make the intended reader's job easier? | Task completion, correct decision or qualitative task observation |
| Truth and source integrity | Are literal and strongly implied claims supported? | Claim-to-source audit, source independence and review |
| Narrative identity | Can the reader identify what happened or changed? | Unprompted recall of relevant event(s) |
| Causal calibration | Can the reader distinguish cause, sequence and interpretation? | “What caused the result?” probe and uncertainty rating |
| Coherence and throughline | Do episodes connect in time, meaning and consequence? | Reader accurately reconstructs the relationship |
| Appropriate stakes | Does the audience understand relevance without inflated drama? | Reader correctly describes significance and limits |
| Voice authenticity | Does an attributed speaker recognize their own meaning? | Speaker review and change-diff assessment |
| Fairness and representation | Are people and competing contributions portrayed accurately? | Affected-party / expert editorial review when warranted |
| Ethical/permission fit | Is publication defensible under its risk and jurisdiction? | Consent/rights/risk controls and sign-off as scoped |
| Emotional proportionality | Do feeling and intensity reflect source reality? | No unsupported motive, fear, trauma, triumph or exaggeration |
| Length and information cost | Does each narrative element earn attention? | Comparative comprehension/time; deletion test |
| Outcome | Was the intended change in understanding/behavior achieved? | Named relevant metric, not click/view alone |
| Maintainability | Can facts, permissions and present-tense claims be updated? | Source and date ownership, change triggers |

### Four verdicts

`PASS`: no unresolved material truth/ethics/fairness/reader-job defect. `REVISE`: identified repairable deficiency. `HOLD`: material uncertainty or specialist review pending. `REJECT`: structural reliance on fabrication, deceptive implications or otherwise unacceptable risk.

A high score in engagement **cannot** override a `HOLD/REJECT` from the truth gate.

## 19.2 Release gates

**Gate 0 — Purpose and form.** Audience and need defined; narrative's incremental job over direct explanation identifiable; genre/truth mode declared.

**Gate 1 — Evidence and rights.** Source status, relevant consent/usage rights, uncertainty and causal boundaries accessible for material nonfiction claims; approved brand inputs collected where relevant.

**Gate 2 — Architecture.** Subject/event/temporal progression/throughline coherent enough for audience; no framework-required fabricated beats.

**Gate 3 — Construction.** Meaningful opening, justified detail, honest pacing/ending, distinctive voice, correct localization and WR-00 writing-quality requirements.

**Gate 4 — Factual and ethical verification.** Direct quotes, dates, motives, numbers, causal inferences, implied typicality, privacy, synthetic material and omission pass risk-appropriate independent challenge. This is a **blocking gate** for consequential nonfiction.

**Gate 5 — Reader/scenario validation.** Test the actual reader task and misleading inferences when importance or uncertainty warrants; compare non-story when form choice is material. Author-side review is insufficient for claims of externally demonstrated effectiveness.

**Gate 6 — Release and maintenance.** Accountable owner, version, evidence cutoff, authorization, correction route, refresh triggers and relevant audience feedback are in place.

## 19.3 Working definition of ready

An ordinary narrative assignment is ready when the intended audience, outcome, story/non-story rationale, factual or fictional mode, source access, principal disclosure constraints, source of approved strategic claims, responsible editor and required review depth are known enough to begin. It is not necessary to know the final theme before interviewing or drafting; research may legitimately change the story question.

## 19.4 Working definition of done

No known material unsupported or misleading claim remains; actual source status and uncertainty are preserved; the chosen structure fits the audience and genre; no unnecessary material was fabricated for drama; the correct specialists reviewed material risk; a release owner can explain why story was chosen over a simpler alternative; version and update responsibility are assigned. `Done for publication` is **not** `scientifically proven optimal`.

## 19.5 Compact per-story checklist (human + AI)

```text
[ ] I can name the reader and what they need to understand or do.
[ ] I asked whether a direct answer, table, timeline or explanation is better.
[ ] I know what event(s) actually happened and what changed.
[ ] I can trace material facts, numbers, quotes and character claims to sources.
[ ] I have not converted mere chronology or attribution into proven causation.
[ ] Intention, conflict, stakes, emotion and outcomes are proportionate and sourced.
[ ] The throughline connects past, present and any labeled possible future.
[ ] Every scene detail/dialogue/inner thought is supported or clearly fictional.
[ ] I have preserved the speaker's authentic voice and working passages.
[ ] I have not fabricated vulnerability, victory, customer proof or a moral.
[ ] A single unusual case is not misleadingly presented as typical.
[ ] Privacy, rights, permissions and harms have been considered in context.
[ ] Readers can distinguish fact, memory, interpretation and scenario.
[ ] Material contrary context and unresolved uncertainty are visible.
[ ] The opening or hook does not hide material information.
[ ] I checked what readers might falsely infer from the whole account.
[ ] Relevant external claims/positioning have authorized specialist owners.
[ ] Editing and testing are proportionate to consequence.
[ ] Source owner, publication authority, revision history and update trigger exist.
[ ] The story is more useful than its simpler alternative; otherwise I can omit it.
```

## 19.6 V2 two-layer publication gate: facts AND implications

A factual verifier asks: “Can each meaningful event, quote and number be traced?” An implication verifier asks: “What claim would a reasonable person take away from the sequence, structure and omissions?” Both can fail independently.

| Gate | Fail condition | Minimum repair | Release result |
|---|---|---|---|
| `SOURCE` | High-salience real claim lacks adequate evidence | Remove/attribute/collect evidence | HOLD if consequential |
| `CAUSALITY` | Story asserts more than timeline/analysis supports | Restrict causal language, show alternatives | HOLD until fixed |
| `REPRESENTATION` | People/teams deprived of material agency, false consensus | Correct roles and material perspectives | REVISE/HOLD |
| `TRUTH_MODE` | Composite, reconstruction or future scenario passes as witnessed fact | Label/restructure or reject form | HOLD/REJECT |
| `CONSENT_RIGHTS` | Material use unauthorized under applicable context | Obtain required permission or remove | HOLD/REJECT |
| `READER` | Narrative delays necessary decision, implies misleading typicality | Direct-first, brief example, caveat | REVISE |
| `VOICE` | AI edits alter speaker's intended meaning or fabricate emotion | Restore original and narrow edits | REVISE |
| `OUTCOME` | Engagement asserted to prove comprehension or commercial lift | State diagnostic metric only; evaluate actual outcome | QUALIFY/INCONCLUSIVE |

**No compensation across gates.** A compelling emotional score cannot offset a fabricated quotation; a low-risk style weakness cannot be treated as equal severity to public harm. A story that passes factual verification but materially misleads through arrangement does **not** pass WR-02. [N-REUTERS][P-MPS]

# 20. Testing, evaluation and outcome measurement

## 20.1 Research question before metric

Decide whether you want the reader to **notice**, **orient**, **understand**, **remember**, **accurately infer a causal relationship**, **empathize**, **trust appropriately**, **consider an option**, **take action**, or **support a collective decision**. These are different outcomes. A high “story was inspiring” score is not a measure of accurate causal understanding; a strong completion rate may coexist with mistaken beliefs about typical results.

## 20.2 Reader-test probes

For an About story, ask readers to explain the person's role and distinctive relevant experience without prompting. For a case story, ask “What happened, what can and cannot be attributed to the intervention, and would you expect a similar result?” For a change narrative, ask employees to identify what is changing, what remains undecided, and what action is required. For a future vision, ask participants which statements are verified current facts and which are conditional possibilities. For editorial nonfiction, probe what readers believe happened, which sources underlie that belief, and what remains unknown.

Tests should look for **false inference** as well as literal recall. Readers remembering “The founder almost went bankrupt” when the text never says it but strongly implies it is a narrative quality failure, not a technical pass on wording.

## 20.3 Story versus matched direct explanation

When attempting to estimate the *incremental causal value* of story form, create two candidates with substantively comparable facts, evidence and meaningful claims. Avoid designing a richly informative narrative and a weak, vague direct control—that compares information volume and craft quality, not narrative mode.

Suggested evaluation record:

```yaml
experiment_id: WR02-EVAL-001
reader_job: accurate_understanding_of_change
audience_population: defined
comparison:
  A: narrative_version
  B: information_equivalent_direct_version
assignment: randomized_if_feasible
primary_outcome: accurately_identify_change_and_uncertainty
secondary_outcomes:
  - retention_after_defined_delay
  - time_to_find_key_information
  - relevance_or_engagement
  - intention_or_action_if_material
guardrails:
  - misleading_causal_inferences
  - misunderstanding_of_typicality
  - privacy_disclosure_harm
  - factual_accuracy
predefined_decision_threshold: based_on_consequence_and_minimum_meaningful_gain
analysis_population: assigned_users_or_defined_qualitative_sample
measurement_limits: declared
result: PASS | FAIL | INCONCLUSIVE | NOT_RUN
```

This is an **example schema**. Do not invent a universal sample size, uplift expectation, confidence threshold or testing cadence. A small formative study identifies misunderstanding; a randomized study can estimate local causal effects if its design is sound; a pre/post business chart without valid comparison rarely isolates copy from channel, audience and product changes. [P-MEAS][N-RAH25]

## 20.4 Measurement ladder (do not confuse tiers)

| Signal | Legitimate use | Cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Scroll depth/read time/video completion | Exposure/engagement diagnostic | Correct understanding or persuasion |
| Like/share/comment | Visible reactions/distribution | Truthfulness, representativeness or qualified demand |
| Recall, paraphrase, comprehension | What readers retained/understood in test | Durable choices or commercial return |
| False-inference probes | Potential narrative distortion | Universal bias-free interpretation |
| Appropriate trust, belief calibration | Whether readers understand certainty and source limits | Purchase or lasting behavior |
| Enquiry/signup/decision action | Observed intended behavior with tracking | Incremental impact without a counterfactual |
| Randomized lift or strong causal evaluation | Estimated local effect on specified outcomes | Universal performance for all future readers/contexts |
| Downstream retention/outcome | Longer-term value and guardrail | Full attribution to a story without identification |

## 20.5 Follow-up and maintenance

For evergreen public stories, schedule review by consequence and rate of change, not by calendar ritual alone. Founder bios, product claims, customer outcome cases and future commitments have distinct freshness risks. When a material error is found, correct the public artifact and linked derivatives; preserve a revision record appropriate to publication type. Where a source is withdrawn, reassess every claim dependent on it.

## 20.6 V2 evaluation portfolio and interpretation rules

**Level A — Formative correctness review.** One or more representative readers explain what they think happened, what the outcome means and what remains unverified. Useful for finding defects, not estimating population-level uplift. For high-risk real-person stories, include a relevant specialist/cultural or affected-party review where warranted.

**Level B — Matched comparison.** Present an equally factually complete narrative and explanatory counterpart. Predefine what counts as a successful answer; assess comprehension, causal inference, time and appropriate trust. Disclose any remaining differences in length, visuals, production quality, speaker, brand cues and emotional appeal. A narrative with six helpful facts against a bare two-fact control does not isolate narrative form. [N-RAH25][N-STAT22]

**Level C — Behavioral experiment.** When investment is material, randomize assignments when feasible and appropriate. Preserve the actual denominator, report attrition, delayed effects and multiple endpoints. Match deployment and measurement conditions; do not interpret platform conversion-credit as incrementality. [P-MEAS]

**Level D — Longitudinal/organizational evaluation.** Change stories may intend shared understanding of choices, willingness to provide feedback, operational preparedness or cross-team coordination. Measure concrete understanding, actual decisions, concerns surfaced and implementation quality separately. Employee satisfaction or management's preferred summary is not proof of adoption or culture change. [N-AOM16][P-MPS]

### Claims an evaluation is not allowed to make

- A positive narrative-vs-no-message result proves superiority over a useful direct explanation.
- A 69% favorable study count in a scoping review means a 69% chance of success on our next story.
- A `g=0.55` average memory advantage estimates our site's conversion-rate lift.
- A six-arcs computational study proves six universal human plots.
- More transportation/tears/read time automatically means truth, decision quality or ethical quality.
- An A/B result with one audience/medium and no preregistered meaningful outcome proves the framework is universally optimal.

### Decision rules for V2 field pilots

`KEEP_STORY`: improves a primary outcome enough to justify maintenance/cost without worsened falsity/harm. `USE_DIRECT`: narrative adds no meaningful reader outcome or harms speed/accuracy. `HYBRID`: direct message plus short narrative yields useful clarity/connection. `REVISE`: structural problems can be repaired. `INCONCLUSIVE`: evidence insufficient. `STOP`: truth/privacy/ethics harm. No general minimum uplift is invented; thresholds depend on the defined decision and its consequences. [N-MAR21][N-RAH25][P-MPS]

## 20.7 V2 methods for detecting false inference

Use a **blind paraphrase**, a **source-separation question** and a **counterfactual probe**. Example for a customer case: “What improvement did the customer observe?”; “What evidence proves the product caused it?”; “Would you expect the same result for most other customers?” This reveals whether readers overgeneralize without requiring them to know that the text is under review. Record verbatim answers, sample characteristics and the source of misunderstanding. Do not coach respondents toward the desired interpretation before testing. [N-PNAS14][P-MEAS]

## 22.3 V2 independent pilot protocol (not executed)

Choose a named reviewer/editor who did not write WR-02, and recruit at least the diverse, relevant participant types needed for the task: an ordinary reader, one trained editor/source reviewer, and specialist representation for regulated or vulnerable-subject cases. A fixed universal pilot count would itself be folklore. The inherited 12 scenarios are coverage minimums *for this project*, not an empirically optimal sample-size threshold. [P-MPS]

1. **Pre-register** the task and which WR-02 controls should fire for each candidate. Randomly vary source ambiguities and case selection in simulation when feasible.
2. **Blind the verifier** to whether the candidate was human or AI-written for initial source/implication QA where practical.
3. **Use source oracles** with known real/synthetic factual ground truth. Never run high-risk simulations with live private customer data unless explicitly authorized.
4. **Measure implementation quality:** correct mode selection, source-causality labeling, privacy handling, time/cost, override/escalation fidelity and false-positive intervention burden.
5. **Measure reader outcomes:** accurate paraphrase, comprehension, false inference, time to find information, credible uncertainty understanding and, where material, delayed decision/behavior.
6. **Compare to V1 and direct formats** when the change or story choice claims measurable improvement. If the new process adds effort without meaningful benefit, simplify or roll back the unnecessary step.
7. **Assign defects:** `BLOCKER` (fabrication, harmful rights/privacy breach, unsupported consequential efficacy); `MAJOR` (false causality, material misleading typicality, broken execution); `MINOR` (navigation/style inefficiency); `OBSERVATION` (non-defect preference).
8. **Close and retest:** record evidence, correction, reviewer, version and regression scenario; no `TESTED` before actual non-author execution evidence, no `VALIDATED` before representative field fitness and MPS gates.

**Causal test design:** an RCT of headline or narrative form must predefine the actual desired outcome and differentiate assignment, exposure, response and measured action. A qualitative test cannot estimate population lift; an A/B test cannot establish a universal theory of storytelling. [N-RAH25][P-MEAS]

# 23. Implementation, governance and maintenance

## 23.1 Adoption model

**Owner** maintains the master WR-02 source and schedules review. **Editors/writers** own narrative truth, structure and quality decisions; **subject/domain specialists** own technical factual inputs; **Brand Strategy** owns positioning and approved value/proof; **WR-01** owns sales persuasion/claims substantiation; **privacy/legal/publisher authorities** decide scoped policy constraints; **research/measurement** owns outcome identification and test validity. An AI tool may assist within its authorized access but does not become a person, witness, legal authorizer or factual owner.

Adoption begins with a small portfolio of stories and non-stories. Provide the compact mode decision and safety checklist to content creators, train on the event-vs-cause distinction, establish a correction route and run one independent execution using a real controlled brief. Avoid making every short anecdote go through an elaborate R3 dossier; increase controls for claims and harms that matter.

## 23.3 Exception and waiver policy

A house style deviation is permissible if it better serves reader, voice or genre, is consistent with truthful representation and is approved where necessary. An exception MUST NOT be used to authorize fabrications, misrepresentation, material privacy violations or misleading commercial claims. For a high-consequence deviation, document rationale, scope, owner, expiry/revisit condition and compensating review. “The story feels stronger” is not an exception rationale for breaking factual integrity.

## 23.4 Maintenance events

Reassess WR-02 after a stronger synthesis contradicts a central effect claim, MPS/WR-00/Brand Strategy changes, a major source is corrected/withdrawn, a misleading real-person narrative produces an incident, AI generation changes materially, a previously effective pattern systematically confuses readers, a new platform or jurisdictional rule constrains representation, or test evidence shows the protocol causes disproportionate review overhead.

A new academic article or bestselling book is not itself a trigger to adopt its framework as universal doctrine. It is an occasion to ask whether relevant evidence materially updates an identified decision.

## 23.5 V2 ownership and correction operating loop

**Responsibility split:** The content editor owns the disclosed factual structure and choice of mode. **Two-track maintenance:** (a) **evergreen narrative principles** reviewed on scheduled cadence, and (b) **publication-specific fast facts** reviewed on event/claim triggers: product capabilities, employment status, founder/team membership, customer approval, current results, privacy requests, changed source facts, or new legal requirements. An evergreen standard cannot freeze a current business result. [P-MPS][P-WR00]

**Exception log:** Every exception records a specific rule, owner, reason, impact, alternatives and compensation/revisit. Genre experimentation may waive a style default; it may not turn invented or unverified high-salience content into fact. [P-MPS]

### V2 source triangulation and contradictory signal register

- **Narrative memory:** [N-MAR21] supports an average benefit in studied text tasks; very high `I²` and audience/genre mismatch prevent universal extrapolation.
- **Narrative persuasion:** [N-BRADDOCK16] supports modest persuasion effects, while [N-RAH25] and [N-STAT22] delimit relative superiority under stronger head-to-head comparators.
- **Transportation:** [N-VANLAER][N-THOMAS24][N-GREENAPPEL24] describe mechanisms and moderators, while [N-HET21] demonstrates possible opposite audience responses.
- **Science/health:** [N-DUDLEY23] adds an informative scoping map, but pooled effect/causal confidence cannot be inferred from positive-study counts.
- **Organizational and public narratives:** [N-AOM16][N-HBR23] identify useful issues and methods; [N-GANZ-ETH26] foregrounds ethics/power. No source establishes one universal executive story structure.
- **Personal narrative:** [N-MCADAMS][N-POEL19][N-MCADAMS-BOOK] distinguish correlational identity research from coercive therapeutic claims.
- **Nonfiction source integrity:** [N-REUTERS][N-OHA][N-NIEMANCLARK04][N-DART-INTERVIEW] converge on truthful care but differ in publication rights/consent context.
- **AI:** [N-AICREATE][N-AIHOMO] support possible co-creation benefits and diversity costs in bounded settings; no study grants AI a license to fabricate factual life events.

**What this register does *not* establish:** exhaustive systematic search, replication of proprietary experiments, a universally “best” character/conflict/arc formula, verified legal compliance in all jurisdictions, field usability of WR-02 V2, or reader-outcome superiority over V1. These are explicit limitations, not implied approvals.

### Source integrity caveats

- The evidence register reports source publication facts and **scoped findings**; it does not claim original reanalysis of underlying datasets or comprehensive PRISMA screening.
- Narrative theory and empirical behavior studies answer different questions. Theoretical elegance is not an effect size; effect size is not a proof of ethical fitness.
- Older foundational sources remain relevant to concepts. Fast-moving AI findings and current external policies require periodic review.
- Direct study comparisons must preserve treatment, control, population, genre, medium and outcome definitions before generalizing.

# 25. Canonical glossary and one-page executive card

## 25.1 Glossary

**Narrative:** representation of an event or events in some communicative form; minimal definition varies by school. **Story:** used here interchangeably in ordinary practice, while acknowledging distinct technical traditions. **Event:** an occurrence or state change in the represented world. **Fabula / story time:** events in their represented chronology. **Discourse / telling order:** sequence and manner in which narrator presents them. **Plot:** a selected arrangement and linking of events; not synonymous with all temporal sequence. **Subject/protagonist:** principal person, group or entity followed; not necessarily a hero. **Agency:** capacity or role in bringing about actions/consequences. **Intention:** a goal or desire; in nonfiction, source-sensitive. **Stakes:** credible importance of potential outcomes. **Conflict/tension:** a complication, uncertainty, trade-off or opposed force; not mandatory violence or villain. **Causal link:** supported relation that helps explain an event, distinct from “after.” **Throughline:** selected recurring thread connecting material events to a central question. **Premise:** central situation or proposition explored. **Theme:** recurrent pattern of meaning. **Point of view / focalization:** whose perspective or knowledge frames the account. **Scene:** representation of a bounded particular event using evidence-supported detail. **Exposition:** summarized or explanatory communication across events/context. **Narrative transportation:** psychological involvement in a story world, not proof of truth. **Coda:** a return to the present/frame of telling in certain narrative traditions; optional. **Composite:** combined elements from multiple cases; must be disclosed where one actual case would otherwise be inferred. **Scenario:** hypothetical future possibility with assumptions. **Claim:** statement or strong implication that requires an evidentiary basis appropriate to its consequences.

## 25.2 The one-page WR-02 rule

```text
1. READER: What do they actually need? Is story better than the direct form?
2. TRUTH: What event happened? How do we know? What remains unknown?
3. SUBJECT: Who or what changed? Whose agency and perspective matter?
4. LINKS: Separate time, supported cause, attributed interpretation, theme.
5. STAKES: Explain importance without fictitious suffering or triumph.
6. LINE: Keep one recognizable thread across relevant past and present.
7. FUTURE: Label aspiration, target, plan, forecast and scenario correctly.
8. FORM: Scene only from sourced detail; use exposition without shame.
9. VOICE: Preserve author/speaker, especially during AI editing.
10. FRAMEWORK: Optional diagnostic, never evidence or imposed truth.
11. ETHICS: Respect privacy, fairness, rights, typicality and dissent.
12. ENDING: Say what actually followed, including non-resolution.
13. VERIFY: Check claims AND the overall story's implied meaning.
14. TEST: Comprehension and false inferences before vanity metrics.
15. PRESERVE: Rewrite only to fix a named defect; maintain a source trail.

ALLOWED OUTCOME: NO_STORY.
BLOCKED: invented facts, quotes, crises, motives, proof and causal certainty.
RELEASE: REVIEWED narrative, qualified where material; not auto-VALIDATED.
```

## 26.8 Author-side worked stress checks — examples of applying the rules

The following are **invented test inputs**, clearly labeled as hypotheticals. They test whether the *logic of the playbook* leads to a safe choice. They are **not** empirical examples, external endorsements or evidence that real nonauthor operators can execute this procedure unaided.

**Stress check A — founder asks for a dramatic origin.** Hypothetical evidence: company began with two collaborators and their first product was an ordinary improvement to an internal workflow. The prompt requests: “We nearly went bankrupt and risked everything; make it cinematic.” **Reject** near-bankruptcy and other unverified jeopardy. The appropriate account can explain the observed problem, attempted solution, feedback and iteration without a personal trauma plot. Passing controls: documented event sequence, accurate agency, preserved voice, no unsupported hardship. Failing control: a spectacular but fictitious crisis.

**Stress check B — case-study causation.** Hypothetical source: one customer reports a shorter process after changing tooling *and* hiring a coordinator. The draft asserts “Our platform cut handling time by 40%.” **Block** the causal and quantitative claim unless credible measurement and attribution support it. Possible safe telling: “The customer reported a shorter process after introducing several changes, including the platform,” provided this statement itself is sourced. Test the reader's inferred attribution and typicality; route efficacy/generalization claims to WR-01's evidence rules.

**Stress check C — future company narrative.** Hypothetical board brief: “By 2030 our service will be used in every school.” No feasibility evidence exists. **Rewrite the truth-status**, not the ambition: “Our ambition is to make the service available to schools nationally by 2030; the scale and timeline depend on adoption and funding.” Keep targets, aspirations, forecasts and conditional scenarios distinct, and have the strategic owner approve commitments. A vivid story does not authorize a forecast.

**Stress check D — survivor interview.** Hypothetical journalist has a confidential account involving identifiable third parties and traumatic recollection. **Separate** consent to interview, permission for AI processing, permission to quote, verification, public-interest balancing, and privacy risk. Do not require disclosure of painful detail merely because “show, don't tell” suggests it. For investigative reporting, subject veto and publication authority remain context-dependent and cannot be flattened into a universal permission rule.

**Stress check E — urgent operational information.** Hypothetical page must tell users an appointment has been canceled and how to reschedule. A long brand story is proposed before the actionable details. **Choose `NO_STORY` for the primary message.** Explain cancellation, effects and steps first; a short contextual explanation may be appended if relevant. A narrative loses when it increases time to action or hides essential facts.

**Stress-check verdict:** the decision system returns appropriate `REJECT`, `QUALIFY`, `USE_DIRECT` or `REQUIRE_DOMAIN_REVIEW` branches for these five authored cases. This is a limited internal consistency demonstration; it does not pass the nonauthor field validation gate.
